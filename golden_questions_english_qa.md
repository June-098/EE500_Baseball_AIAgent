Q. How do I hit the outside pitch?

A. Do not let the outside pitch travel deep. The old three-ball teaching (inside pitch far out front, middle pitch at the plate, outside pitch deep) is outdated. Pro hitters meet every pitch in about the same contact window: near the plane of the front foot after it lands. On the outside pitch you change the barrel's direction, not your contact depth. A right-handed hitter drives it toward right-center.

Why not deep: against 95-98 mph there is very little reaction time. Waiting until the ball reaches the catcher's side of the plate makes the barrel late. You get swings and misses, fouls straight back, weak opposite-field flies, or contact off the handle.

How to cover it:
1. Land in a balanced position.
2. Keep the hands connected to the body.
3. Let the back shoulder work toward the pitch.
4. Use the legs, back knee, and back hip to rotate.
5. Turn the barrel behind the ball. Use a south-to-north (Ferris-wheel) path, not an east-to-west swing around the body.
6. Keep going through the outer part of the plate.
7. Drive the ball to the middle or the opposite-field gap.

Drill: the BOSU Ball Drill. Put a BOSU trainer between your legs as a guide (do not stand on it). Set a tee low and outside. Load into the back hip, land, drive the back knee and back hip toward the hitting area, let the back shoulder work toward the pitch, and turn the barrel behind the ball toward the middle or opposite field. For a reference, watch this video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bMPY5FJup6g) at 2:47. Then move to front toss: start low and away, then middle-away and high-away, and adjust the barrel angle to each height.

Avoid reaching with the arms, pulling the front shoulder away, cutting across the ball, and losing balance. Cue: "One contact window, three barrel directions."

Q. How do I hit a curveball?

A. Curveballs are hard for three reasons. They move below or away from where your eyes first expect them. They arrive slower than the fastball, so early hands make you swing early, reach, roll over, or pull off. And the breaking ball is often the pitcher's best weapon.

Four mechanics fix most of it:
1. Match the barrel to the pitch's path. A downward-breaking pitch needs a stronger hip hinge, more shoulder tilt, a lower barrel entry, and a controlled low-to-high path through contact. This is not a big uppercut. Follow the ball with your eyes while the head stays stable. Do not collapse the whole body toward a low pitch.
2. Stay compact without pushing the hands. Be short into the zone and long through it. Do not chop down at a breaking ball.
3. Keep your body direction. Do not pull the front shoulder away from a ball breaking toward the outer edge. Let the back shoulder work toward the pitch.
4. "The way you come in is the way you go out." A barrel that enters behind the ball on the pitch's plane stays in the zone longer.

Separation buys you the time. Let the lower body stride forward while the upper body stays loaded, and land with the hands still near the rear shoulder. Because the hands have not fired yet, you can delay the final barrel turn when the slower pitch comes. Start the turn with a "roll" of the rear elbow and barrel, not a hand push. A push makes a steep "V" path. A roll makes a long "Nike swoosh" path.

Drills:
- The Roll Drill: hold a basketball near the rear shoulder, stride while keeping it there, land without committing the hands, then rotate and let the rear elbow and barrel work into the slot. Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xjtune7wX5s) at 2:10. Move from dry swings to tee to front toss.
- Ferris-Wheel and Closed-Net drills (Episodes 4 and 1) for barrel plane and compactness.
- Recognition progression: watch breaking balls without swinging, call the pitch early, then alternate fastballs and breaking balls in front toss. Swing only at ones that finish in your zone. Take the ones that finish below it.

Avoid committing the hands before you recognize the pitch, chopping down, letting the head drift, and trying to lift every breaking ball.

Q. What is launch angle and what should mine be?

A. Launch angle is the vertical direction of the ball right after contact, measured in degrees. It describes the ball, not the bat. The bat's direction before contact is a different number called attack angle.

General ranges:
- Below 0 degrees: driven into the ground.
- 0-10 degrees: ground ball or low line drive.
- 10-20 degrees: line drive.
- 20-30 degrees: strong fly ball or home run, if exit velocity (ball speed off the bat, in mph) is high enough.
- Very high angles: easy fly balls or pop-ups.

What should yours be? There is no single magic number. Launch angle only means something next to exit velocity, contact quality, and direction. A ball hit about 100 mph at about 25 degrees has strong home-run potential. Most hitters are not near 100 mph. For many hitters, steady hard line drives around 10-20 degrees (about 15 degrees) are a better goal than trying to lift every ball to 20-25 degrees. The notes do not give a separate high school target.

How to get it: launch angle should be the result of a good swing, not something you force. Load into the rear hip, land balanced, turn the barrel behind the ball, and get on plane early. Getting on plane means matching the barrel to the pitch's slightly downward path before contact, then keeping it there through the zone. Hit near the center of the ball and keep going toward your target.

Avoid scooping or leaning back to make lift. Also avoid chopping down ("get on top of the ball"). A steep downswing gives the bat and ball only a short moment together. It produces chopped grounders, rollovers, and pop-ups.

Q. What is attack angle and how is it different from launch angle?

A. Attack angle is the angle of the barrel's path right before contact, in degrees. Launch angle is the angle of the ball right after contact. One describes the bat. The other describes the ball.

- Attack angle: direction of the bat, before and during contact.
- Launch angle: direction of the batted ball, right after contact.
- Exit velocity: speed of the batted ball after contact.

Positive attack angle: the barrel moves forward and slightly upward into contact. It is a smooth, shallow rise, not a big uppercut. It matches the downward path of the pitch, keeps the barrel in the zone longer, and gives more room for solid contact. It produces hard line drives and fly balls from gap to gap.

Negative attack angle: the barrel moves downward into the ball ("swing down," "chop"). It crosses the pitch's path instead of riding with it, like two roads meeting at an intersection. Results are topped grounders, weak contact, rollovers, pop-ups if you cut under the ball, and a tiny margin for timing errors.

The two numbers are linked but not the same. With the same positive attack angle, launch angle depends on where the barrel meets the ball:
- Above center: ground ball.
- Center: hard line drive.
- Slightly below center: line drive with lift.
- Too far below center: pop-up.

So a positive attack angle is only half of it. You still have to square the ball. Do not chase launch angle by dropping the back shoulder, lowering the hands, leaning back, or scooping. The notes give no target number for attack angle; they describe a small, positive angle.

To improve it: start balanced, load into the back hip without dropping the barrel, land in an adjustable position, turn the barrel onto the pitch's plane, keep it through the zone, and square the ball.

Q. How should I load my hips before the swing?

A. The hip load (also called the hip hinge, the gather, or getting into an athletic position) puts your weight and tension into the rear hip before the swing. It gives you two things: balance, so you can hold still for a moment while you track the pitch, and power, because the rear glute and hamstring are ready to rotate the body.

Hinge at the hip. Do not only bend the knees.
1. Start in a comfortable stance.
2. Shift your balance into the rear leg.
3. Bend both knees slightly.
4. Move the hips back, like closing a car door with your rear hip. Think of pulling the rear hip toward the dugout behind you.
5. Keep your back strong and neutral. Do not round the lower back.
6. Hold it and feel the rear glute and hamstring turn on.
7. Stay balanced, with your weight inside the rear foot.

It looks like the start of a Romanian deadlift (RDL). The chest may tilt forward a little, like Mike Trout or Joey Votto. Do not go too deep.

Two mistakes to avoid:
- Sway: the whole body drifts over or past the rear foot. The head moves back, weight goes to the outside of the rear foot, and you have to rush forward to recover.
- Quad-dominant: hips stay tall, chest stays upright, the rear knee drives forward over the toes, and you feel nothing in the glute or hamstring.

Q. How do I separate my hands during the swing?

A. Separate with a "scap load," not by pushing your hands away from your body.

Separation means the lower body strides toward the pitcher while the upper body stays loaded. The wrong way is to "walk away from your hands": the hands drift back or out, the shaft of the bat gets far from the body, and you lose barrel consistency.

The right way:
1. Get into your back hip as you stride.
2. Load the rear shoulder blade (scapula) slightly toward the spine. The hands move back with it.
3. When the front foot lands (the 50-50 position), keep the bat shaft close to the rear shoulder, with the knob pointing toward the catcher.
4. Stay square to the plate. Do not dump the front shoulder in.
5. Keep the rear elbow up. Do not let it drop.

Quick check for a coach or parent: from a front view at landing, you should see the rear elbow behind the hitter's back. If you cannot see it, the hitter is not separating correctly.

Why it matters: Mike Trout and Mookie Betts keep the bat close to their center of mass. Their swings are more consistent, and they handle both fastballs and off-speed pitches better. Ronald Acuña Jr. gets his hands farther from his body for bat speed, and he goes through more slumps.

Practice it with the Roll Drill: hold a basketball near the rear shoulder, stride while it stays there, land without committing the hands, then rotate. Do not over-stretch the hands behind you. That creates tension and disconnects the arms from the torso.

Q. How do I hit a high pitch?

A. Keep the same swing and change its angle. A low pitch needs more shoulder tilt and a more upward barrel path. A high pitch needs less shoulder tilt, a flatter back shoulder, and a flatter barrel path that turns straight through the height of the ball. Think of the barrel as an extension of your shoulders.

How: make a controlled move forward and land balanced. Start the turn with the legs and hips. Let the back shoulder work toward and through the pitch, with the torso and barrel connected. Do not reach up with the hands or stand up out of your stance. Keep your hip hinge.

What the Ferris wheel is: many hitters swing flat around the body, an east-to-west or "merry-go-round" swing. That barrel cuts across the zone. For a right-handed hitter it races toward the left side, which makes pulled grounders and hooked fouls. The Ferris-wheel swing is a south-to-north path. The barrel comes from behind the body into the hitting zone, then moves forward through the ball with a controlled upward direction. Picture a Ferris wheel turning toward the pitcher instead of a merry-go-round spinning around you. It is not a steep uppercut. On a high pitch the same idea applies, with a flatter shoulder angle.

Drills:
- Drop-Bat Drill (Ferris wheel, credited to Craig Wallenbach): take your normal stance. On an easy front toss, let the barrel settle into its working position (do not let go of the bat). Turn the barrel into the pitch's path, from behind the ball, forward and slightly up through contact. Start slow. Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Z5r9275-yM) at 3:10.
- PVC Pipe Drill: put a light PVC pipe (about 2 inches wide) across your shoulders. Use tennis balls only. A partner tosses to different heights. Stay balanced with the legs and hip hinge, turn the torso, and point the back shoulder at the ball so the pipe travels through the ball's plane. Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AyXaOtquLck) at 1:52.
- Tee Drill: start the tee mid-zone and raise it step by step. Keep the top hand strong, keep barrel lag, match the back-shoulder angle to the ball's height, and drive it on a line.

Discipline: as a guide, do not chase above about belly-button height. Avoid a steep uppercut on high pitches, dropping the back shoulder, standing up, and lifting the hands on their own.

Q. What is the Ferris Wheel drill?

A. In the notes, the Ferris wheel is a swing path, and the Drop-Bat Drill is how you train it.

The problem it fixes: many hitters swing flat around the body. This is the east-to-west or "merry-go-round" swing. The barrel cuts across the hitting zone. For a right-handed hitter it races toward the left side after contact, which causes pulled ground balls and sharply hooked fouls.

The Ferris-wheel (south-to-north) path: the barrel works from behind the body into the hitting zone, then forward through the ball with a controlled upward direction. A Ferris wheel turns toward the pitcher. A merry-go-round spins around you. It does not mean a steep uppercut. The barrel turns into the pitch's path and keeps moving forward.

The Drop-Bat Drill, step by step:
1. Take your normal batting stance.
2. A coach or partner gives an easy front toss.
3. As the ball is tossed, let the barrel settle into its working position while you keep control of the bat.
4. Turn the barrel into the pitch's path.
5. The barrel goes from behind the ball, forward through the zone, and slightly upward through contact.
6. Start slow. Speed up only once you can repeat it safely.

"Drop the bat" does not mean letting go of the bat. It means letting the barrel settle before you turn it through the zone. Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Z5r9275-yM) at 3:10. The drill is credited to hitting instructor Craig Wallenbach.

What it builds: the ball travels into a better contact area, you stop rushing the hands, fewer pulled grounders and hooked fouls, you can handle inside, middle, and outside pitches, and you stay balanced and square to the pitcher.

The Mike Trout link: in batting practice Trout drives his first round to right-center and right field, then works the middle, then a few to left-center. The lesson is not to copy his swing. It is to stay balanced, stay square, and build a barrel path that uses the whole field.

Q. Why is top hand so important in batting?

A. The top hand is the upper hand on the bat: the right hand for a right-handed hitter, the left hand for a lefty. It is often the dominant hand. It turns the barrel onto the pitch's path and delivers it through the hitting zone. Used well, it gets the barrel into the zone early and keeps it there longer. That cuts down rollovers, pop-ups, fouls, and swings under breaking balls.

Two common mistakes:
1. Hammering down to "get on top" of the ball. The top forearm pronates (the palm turns down) too early. The barrel then cuts steeply across the pitch instead of riding through it. Results: topped grounders, weak rollovers to the pull side, hooked fouls, a short hitting zone, less carry, and trouble with the outside slider.
2. Disconnecting the hands. The hitter tries to make bat speed by pushing the top hand away from the rear shoulder. The arms work apart from the body and the barrel leaves its track. It feels fast but is not useful bat speed. Results: pop-ups, fouls, whiffs, contact off the end, and trouble adjusting to breaking balls and changeups.

The correct move: "Connect, roll, and deliver the pizza."
1. Connect: during the load and stride, keep the top hand near the rear shoulder. Connected means the hand, arm, shoulder, and torso move together. It does not mean pressed rigidly against the shoulder. At landing the hand is still back.
2. Roll: once you decide to swing, the lower body and torso turn. The rear elbow works down and around as the barrel turns behind you into the slot. This is not rolling the wrists over the ball.
3. Deliver the pizza: through contact, keep the top palm facing up, as if carrying a pizza (supination). At contact: top palm up, bottom palm down, hands connected, space between the forearms, barrel moving through the ball. After contact the hands turn over naturally. The goal is to delay the rollover, not to stop it.

How it shows up in hitting: you get on plane earlier, stay through contact longer, control the barrel on every location, adjust to off-speed, avoid early rollover, and drive the ball up the middle. Practice with the Roll Drill (Episode 9) and the Direction Drill (Episode 7).

Q. Where should my contact point be?

A. Your contact point should be about the same for every pitch: a window near the plane of your front foot after it lands. Picture a vertical window that starts at the front foot. That is your one reliable reference.

The old way had three depths: inside pitch far out front, middle pitch at the plate, outside pitch deep. That is outdated. Reaching far out front on an inside pitch makes the barrel turn sharply and hook the ball foul. It also makes you cheat for the inside fastball, so a changeup away beats you. Letting a 95-98 mph outside pitch get too deep makes you late.

What changes by location is the barrel's direction, not the depth:
- Inside: turn tighter, hands close, drive it to the pull-side gap (left-center for a righty).
- Middle: drive it through center field.
- Outside: let the barrel work to the opposite field (right-center for a righty).

Cue: "One contact window, three barrel directions."

Steps: land before the final barrel turn, set the front-foot contact plane, adjust the barrel's direction, and keep going through the ball after contact.

This does not mean falling onto your front leg. It describes where the ball is relative to your body. Moving up or back in the box does not change it either. Moving back gives a bit more time. Moving up lets you meet the pitch before it moves as much. Either way, the front foot is still your reference.

Q. How do I stay connected with my lead arm?

A. The lead arm is the arm closest to the pitcher. Its hand is usually the bottom hand on the bat: the left hand for a right-handed hitter. Think of it as the steering wheel. Your body is the engine. Great bat speed does not help if the steering pulls the barrel off its path.

The lead arm's job is to keep the hands connected to the torso, guide the barrel into the pitch's plane, control the swing's direction, keep the hands from leaving the body too early, and keep you "inside" the ball. Inside the ball means the barrel comes from behind the ball. It does not mean pinning the hands to the body. The arm stays connected but not stiff or locked.

The common mistake is pushing the hands. The front foot lands, the hands disconnect, the lead arm pushes forward, the barrel stays behind the hands, and you try to force it to the ball late. That narrow path cuts across the pitch. It causes weak grounders, pull-side rollovers, hooked fouls, lower bat speed, and trouble with off-speed.

The better sequence:
1. Load into the rear hip.
2. Keep the hands near the rear shoulder.
3. Stride and land balanced.
4. Rotate with the lower body and torso.
5. Let the rear shoulder and rear elbow turn the barrel into the slot.
6. Keep the lead hand connected to the body's rotation.
7. Guide the barrel through the ball.
8. Release the barrel naturally after the body turns toward the pitcher.

The body's rotation carries the hands. The lead hand steers.

Willy Adames example: before the fix, his hands separated from his body and left a visible gap. His swing worked around his body, an east-to-west path, instead of entering from behind the ball. After he connected the lead arm, hands, and torso, he stayed inside the ball, stopped pushing his hands, got the barrel into the zone earlier, stayed through contact longer, hit more to the middle, and rolled over less. The fix came from letting the lead arm move with the body, not from making it stiff.

Drill: the Roll Drill (Episode 9) trains this connection.

Q. How do I get on plane with the ball?

A. Getting on plane means turning the barrel so its path starts matching the incoming pitch's path before contact, then keeping it on that path as long as possible. A pitch travels slightly downward into the plate. The barrel should enter that path and ride with it, not cross it for one instant. This gives you a longer contact window, more time to adjust to off-speed, and a better chance to drive the ball.

The G-Swing sequence in one line: "Scap load, roll into the slot, create lag, and accelerate the barrel through the pitch."

Step by step:
1. Begin balanced and connected. Athletic stance, hands near the rear shoulder.
2. Load the rear scapula. As you stride, pull the rear shoulder blade slightly toward the spine. At landing the hands are near the rear shoulder, the rear elbow is behind the body, the bat is close to your center, and you are balanced.
3. Roll the top forearm. It turns the barrel behind you into the pitch's plane, palm up like serving a pizza. This is not rolling the wrists over the ball.
4. Roll the barrel into the slot. The slot is the connected spot where the rear elbow works near your side, the hands stay connected, and the barrel turns behind the hands.
5. Create barrel lag. The barrel briefly trails the hands and the turning body, ready to accelerate. Do not make lag by dragging the hands forward.
6. Accelerate the barrel forward through the zone and through the field, not down at the ball. Finish strong.

Avoid three common cues taken the wrong way: "be short to the ball" (taking the hands straight at the ball makes a tiny window), "get on top of the ball" (palm down, chopping, negative attack angle), and "take the knob to the ball" (hands move first, barrel gets left behind). Check your video for the wrong path: the hands move first, the top palm turns down, the barrel gets steep, cuts across the pitch, and leaves the zone fast.

Why it helps against off-speed: on plane, the barrel stays available longer, so you can delay the final acceleration and still square a changeup or a breaking ball moving down and away.

Q. What is the good directivity of bat path?

A. Good directivity means the barrel, and so the ball, travels toward the part of the field you intend instead of cutting across toward the pull side.

What it looks like on the bat path: the barrel gets onto the pitch's plane before contact, stays through the hitting zone, and then accelerates forward through the field, not down at the ball. The wrong path is the opposite: the hands move first, the top palm turns down, the barrel gets steep, cuts across the pitch, and leaves the zone quickly.

The checkpoint at contact is the money gap: top palm up, bottom palm down, arms connected to the body, and space between the forearms, with the barrel moving through the ball's intended direction. That palm-up, palm-down position delays the rollover. An early rollover (the top hand turning over too soon) makes the barrel cut across the ball and produces weak pull-side grounders or hooked fouls. Do not hold the position after contact. The forearms turn over naturally once the barrel is through the ball.

How to train it: the top-hand and bottom-hand drills. The body creates the direction, the shoulder stays connected, and the hand finishes the action. To send the ball to center, land balanced, move the back shoulder through center field, and release toward the middle.

Avoid: rolling the top hand before contact, pulling with only the arms, pushing the bottom hand away from the body, letting the hands go before the lower body turns, and spinning so hard that the barrel cuts to the pull side right away.

Q. Why do I keep popping the ball up?

A. Pop-ups happen when the barrel meets the ball too far below its center. The launch angle is very high but the exit velocity is low. Coaches usually say you are "under the ball" or "off the plane."

Common causes:
1. Swinging down. A steep downswing crosses the ball's path for only a moment. A small timing miss and you clip under it. Hitting down also creates too much backspin, so the ball goes straight up.
2. Chasing lift. Dropping the back shoulder, lowering the hands, leaning back, or scooping puts the barrel under the ball. A very high launch angle can simply be a weak pop-up.
3. Hands going first. When the hands move before the barrel turns into place, the barrel gets steep and leaves the zone quickly, so timing errors turn into mishits.

Where you hit the ball matters:
- Too high on the ball: ground ball.
- Center: hard line drive.
- Slightly below center: line drive with lift.
- Too far below center: pop-up.

The fix:
- Use a small, positive attack angle. The barrel moves forward and slightly up, a shallow rise, not an uppercut.
- Get on plane early. Scap load, roll the barrel into the slot, create lag, then accelerate through the pitch.
- Aim for solid contact near the center of the ball. Line drives of about 10-20 degrees are a better goal than lifting everything.
- Do not chase useful backspin. It comes from a barrel moving through the ball with contact just below center.

Q. I keep rolling over and hitting weak ground balls to the pull side. What is wrong?

A. You are rolling over. Your top hand turns over before contact, so the barrel cuts across the ball instead of moving through it. The ball gets hit on its top half and goes into the ground toward the pull side.

Why it happens:
1. Top hand hammers down. Trying to "get on top of the ball" makes the top forearm pronate (palm turns down) too early. The barrel gets steep and crosses the pitch's path. Results: topped grounders, pull-side rollovers, hooked fouls, and a short hitting zone.
2. Hands push and the lead arm disconnects. You land and shove both hands toward the pitcher. The barrel stays behind the hands, then gets whipped across the ball late.
3. Contact too far out front. Reaching way out in front, especially on inside pitches, means the barrel is already turning sharply toward the pull side when it meets the ball.

How each part fixes it:
- Swing path: get on plane. Scap load, roll the barrel into the slot, create lag, then accelerate through the ball and through the field, not down at it.
- Lead arm: think steering wheel. Keep it connected to the turning torso so the body carries the hands. The lead hand guides the barrel through the ball's direction instead of pulling it around.
- Top hand: "Connect, roll, deliver the pizza." At contact the top palm is up, the bottom palm is down, with space between the forearms. Let the hands turn over only after the barrel is through the ball.

What it does to contact point and launch angle: a rollover makes contact out front and on top of the ball. That gives a low or negative launch angle (below 0 degrees means the ball is driven into the ground) and weak exit velocity. Fixing the path moves contact back to the front-foot window and lets you meet the ball near center. That turns rollovers into line drives in the 10-20 degree range, aimed up the middle.

Drills: the Roll Drill (Episode 9) for connection and the Direction Drill (Episode 7) for top-hand control.

Q. What is the batting kinetic chain sequence?

A. The kinetic chain runs from the ground up. Each body part turns in order and the bat comes last.

1. Trigger and timing. A first move sets your rhythm and catches the pitch's timing. Examples are a bat wiggle, a foot tap, or a big leg kick. Once it starts, the movement flows from the bottom up: knee rotation, then hip rotation, then rotation of the chest (thorax) and shoulders.
2. Launch (power) position. That sequence brings you to the launch or power position, ready to strike.
3. Execution. When you decide to swing, the front leg lands. Then the foot turns, then the knee, then the hip, and finally the upper body. Only after this chain does the bat swing out.
4. The "back space." About 80% of the mechanism happens before the bat reaches the ball. The keys are the hip hinge (to use the pelvis fully) and upper-lower body separation (twisting the body for maximum torque). Juan Soto is an example: he holds his power on the back-leg axis while keeping a big upper-body twist.
5. Delivery style. Energy moves either by an active forward weight shift (like Yordan Alvarez) or by rotating on the back-leg axis (like Bryce Harper). The back-leg style is common in big home-run hitters like Aaron Judge and Shohei Ohtani.
6. Path and impact. Impact and follow-through are extensions of the flow, not the source of power. Many players describe a "swing down" feeling, but the barrel actually travels a circular, slightly upward path. The feeling comes from the elbow driving down toward the body. Shoulder depth shapes the trajectory. A shallow shoulder entry (Ronald Acuña Jr.) leads to lower launch angles and line drives. A deeper entry (Aaron Judge) lets the hitter get under the ball for higher launch angles and more home runs.

Q. Should I swing down on the ball?

A. No. Do not swing down on the ball.

The pitch is already traveling downward when it reaches you. A downward bat path crosses the pitch's path instead of riding with it, like two roads meeting at an intersection. The bat and ball share the same space for only an instant. That is a negative attack angle. It produces topped and chopped ground balls, pull-side rollovers, weak contact, and pop-ups when the barrel cuts under the ball, because hitting down puts too much backspin on it. It also leaves almost no margin for timing errors. It may work against young defenders. It is not a reliable path to success at higher levels.

The cue "get on top of the ball" is often misread as "turn the top palm down and chop." That is the same mistake.

What to do instead: a small, positive attack angle. The barrel moves forward and slightly upward into contact, matching the pitch's plane and staying in the zone longer. This is a shallow rise, not a big uppercut. Do not scoop or lean back either.

One nuance: some big leaguers say they feel like they swing down. Their barrel actually travels a circular, slightly upward path. The "down" feeling comes from the elbow driving down toward the body, not from the barrel chopping at the ball.

Q. How do I get to the ball strong enough with my bat or hands?

A. Get to the ball with your body, not by throwing your hands at it. Pushing the hands toward the ball feels quick but makes a weak, narrow path.

1. Separate the right way. As you stride, load the rear shoulder blade (scap load) and keep the shaft of the bat close to your rear shoulder, knob toward the catcher. Do not walk the hands away from your body. A bat that stays near your center of mass gives more consistent, stronger swings against both fastballs and off-speed.
2. Let the body carry the hands. Your lower body and torso are the engine. The lead arm is the steering wheel. Keep the lead arm connected to the turning torso and let the rear shoulder and rear elbow turn the barrel into the slot. Then guide the barrel through the ball. If you land and push both hands forward, the barrel stays behind the hands. You lose bat speed and hit weak grounders, rollovers, and hooked fouls.
3. Use a path that stays in the zone. A barrel that moves from low to forward and slightly upward matches the pitch's downward plane and keeps the bat in the hitting zone longer. That gives you more chances at solid contact. It is not a dramatic uppercut.

Q. What should the hip load do?

A. The hip load puts your weight and tension into the rear hip before the swing. It should do two things.

1. Give you balance. Loading into the rear hip lets you stay balanced for a moment while you track the pitch. You control your timing instead of rushing forward.
2. Give you power. When the rear glute and hamstring are engaged, the lower body is ready to rotate hard and create rotational power through the swing.

To make it work, hinge at the hips (move the rear hip back, slight knee bend) instead of bending only at the knees. Keep your weight inside the rear foot. It should not sway the body over the back leg, and it should not turn into a tall, quad-dominant knee bend.

Q. How do I get into atheletic position and prepare to hit the ball?

A. Your athletic position is the hip hinge. It sets your timing and your power before the pitch arrives.

Get into it:
1. Start in a comfortable stance.
2. Shift your balance into the rear leg.
3. Bend both knees slightly.
4. Push the hips back, like closing a car door with your rear hip.
5. Keep the back strong and neutral. The chest can tilt forward a little.
6. Feel the rear glute and hamstring engage.
7. Stay balanced inside the rear foot.

This position lets you hold still for a moment and track the pitch instead of rushing. It also loads the glute and hamstring so the lower body can rotate with force. Do not sway over the back foot. Do not stand tall with the knees pushed forward (quad-dominant).

From there, be ready to swing on plane. The barrel should move from low, forward, and slightly upward to match the pitch's downward path and stay in the zone longer. On a low pitch that path helps you get under the ball for lift and backspin. On a high pitch you can meet the ball a bit farther out front while the barrel is still on its upward plane. The goal is an efficient swing that handles pitches at different heights, not a big uppercut.

Q. How do I hit inside pitches?

A. Let your hands and barrel work through the hitting zone. Do not jam your hands inward or try to yank the ball. The barrel should travel forward with a slight upward turn, like a smooth Ferris wheel.

Meet the ball near the plane of your front foot, the same window as every other pitch. Turn a little tighter and keep your hands close to your body. Your timing decides the direction (right-handed hitter):
- Slightly late: right-center.
- On time: center.
- Slightly early: left-center.
- Too far out front: hooked foul.

The goal is a line drive into the pull-side gap, not a ball hooked foul past third. Reaching far out front also makes you cheat for the inside fastball, and then a changeup away beats you.

Drill: the Closed-Net ("phone booth") Drill. The net makes a tight space around you, like a phone booth.
1. Put a hitting net about three feet behind your normal hitting spot.
2. Put home plate in the center of the box.
3. Take your normal stance.
4. Start with slow-motion swings.
5. Let the barrel turn into the zone and travel through the inside pitch.
6. Do not let the bat hit the net behind you.
7. Practice driving balls to the pull-side gap, center, and the opposite-field gap.

If your barrel gets long or swings too far behind you, it hits the net. The net also keeps your front shoulder from opening early. A compact, well-sequenced swing clears the net and goes through the ball. The goal is not to pull every inside pitch. It is to stay compact, meet the ball at the front-foot plane, and keep it fair.

Q. How do I effectively load my hips before the swing?

A. Load by hinging at the hips, not by bending your knees.

Steps:
1. Start in a comfortable stance.
2. Shift your balance into the rear leg.
3. Bend both knees slightly.
4. Move the hips back as if closing a car door with your rear hip, or pulling the rear hip toward the dugout behind you.
5. Keep the back strong and neutral. Let the chest tilt forward a little from the hips, never by rounding the lower back.
6. Hold it for a moment and feel the rear glute and hamstring engage.
7. Keep the weight inside the rear foot.

It looks like the start of a Romanian deadlift (RDL). Keep it comfortable and athletic, not deep.

What makes it effective: you can stay balanced while you track the pitch and control your timing, and the loaded glute and hamstring give the lower body rotational power.

Check yourself for two mistakes:
- Sway: the head and upper body drift back, weight rolls to the outside of the rear foot, the front leg kicks up, and you have to rush forward. Make the move smaller. Load into the hip instead of leaning over the leg.
- Quad-dominant: hips stay tall, chest upright, rear knee drives over the toes, and you feel nothing in the glute or hamstring. Let the rear hip move back instead.

Q. Pitchers keep pounding my weak spot low and away. Should I give up the low and away and focus on hitting other spots?

A. For most hitters, yes. Concede the cold zone at first and play to your strength. That is the orthodox answer.

Shohei Ohtani is the example. Almost every square of his zone is hot. His one cold zone is low and away, so pitchers work low and away and avoid up and in. That is where his strikeouts come from.

Why giving it up works: a pitcher cannot throw all three strikes to one spot. They have to throw setup pitches. The battle moves into the sequence. They might finish you low and away, use low and away to get ahead and then finish inside, or throw to your favorite spot but jam it for a foul. Your default stays the same: time the fastball, and do not miss the pitch in your own location.

There is a second, harder option for better hitters. Hunt the pitch in your cold zone on purpose, either by drawing the ball there or by predicting when it is coming. Clutch spots often demand this, because no pitcher gives you anything easy when runs are needed.

So start by owning your strengths. Work toward reading when the low-away pitch is coming so you can sit on it.

Q. After two breaking balls miss badly and I am 0-2, should I sit fastball and forget the last pitch?

A. Sitting fastball after two breaking balls miss is the classic read. Forgetting the last pitch is the risky part. The pitcher and catcher build the next pitches on what you just saw.

The source case is Christian Walker against Nathan Eovaldi (2023 World Series). Walker is a strong fastball hitter who is weaker on breaking balls. Texas started with a curveball off the plate away, then a splitter that missed badly. Both were balls, so the count was 2-0 (two balls, no strikes), a hitter's count. Eovaldi had to throw a fastball, and Walker knew it.

What happened next:
- The fastball came, located perfectly down. Walker did nothing with it. 2-1.
- The catcher did not go back to a breaking ball. He changed height. Walker swung expecting a fastball up and got a cutter sliding just off it. 2-2.
- Then a 96 mph four-seamer on the outside corner. With the inside splitter still in his memory, Walker could not time it.

Lessons:
1. Yes, time the fastball in that spot. That is every hitter's default.
2. When the fastball you are sitting on arrives, you cannot miss it. That pitch was Walker's chance.
3. Do not assume the last pitch is gone. The battery changes height and location and uses your memory of earlier pitches to beat your timing.

Note: the source covers a 2-0 count. It does not cover being 0-2 (two strikes) after swinging through breaking balls.

Q. Should I choke up a couple of fingers if I want more contact instead of max exit velo?

A. Yes, if contact is your goal. That is what Cody Bellinger did.

He moved his hands up the handle about two fingers' width. It is not an extreme choke-up. He tried it now and then in late 2022 and used it almost all the time in 2023. The effect: more barrel control and better contact.

His 2023 results, as he traded power for contact:
- Batting average: .210 (2022) to .305 (2023).
- Strikeout rate: about 27% down to about 17%.
- In-zone whiff rate: 22.4% down to 17.8%.
- Off-speed whiff rate: 31.4% down to 15.3%, hitting .412 against off-speed.
- Line-drive rate: 22.2% up to 26.6%.
- Average exit velocity: 89.4 mph down to 86.9 mph.

The lower exit velocity is the cost of the trade, not a problem. He stopped hunting home runs and started hunting contact. He also paired the choke-up with a flatter barrel angle at the trigger, which was the bigger change.

Q. Will laying the barrel flatter at trigger help me catch changeups I keep missing?

A. It can. In Cody Bellinger's case it was the bigger of his two changes, and better handling of off-speed pitches was the biggest reason his average came back.

What changed: right before the barrel turns, his bat used to sit nearly vertical. In 2023 he laid it off closer to flat.

Why it helps:
1. A flatter barrel shows more hitting surface to the ball, so you hit more line drives.
2. It removes a step. The bat head always comes forward as the upper body coils, so a vertical bat has to be flattened during the swing before it can be delivered. From a flatter start, the trigger fires and the barrel turns out from where it already is. No resetting, no pushing the barrel into place. That saves one or two video frames, and that was the decisive reason his average moved so much.

The off-speed numbers: his off-speed whiff rate was cut roughly in half, from 31.4% to 15.3%, and he hit .412 against off-speed pitches. He also choked up about two fingers' width at the same time.

Q. Does tucking my back knee in help contact but make me late on real heaters?

A. Yes to both. That is Jung-Hoo Lee's trade-off.

The inward back knee is the traditional Japanese form. It favors precision over force. From that position the hip rotates out and he turns in place. There is no weight shift. He hits with rotational energy alone.

The upside: the back leg and hip travel a short distance. That lets him keep the contact point deep and hit compactly with impact. It is the source of his elite contact and his adjustment to off-speed.

The downside: premium velocity. A deeper contact point makes the best fastballs hard to cover. About 150 km/h (about 93 mph) is manageable. That is only the MLB average, though. Most teams' top two starters and most main relievers sit above 155 km/h (about 96 mph). "Deeper" here means only one or two balls' width, which is a big difference in baseball.

The comparison: a back knee lined up straight with the ground produces more force. Think of jumping off one leg. Nobody jumps with the knee caving in. It is hard to find an MLB hitter whose back leg is not set in line.

Q. If I open my stance for more power, how do I keep my front shoulder from flying open?

A. That is the exact risk Jung-Hoo Lee ran into. Opening up let his front shoulder come open, and that was most likely why he went back to his old form.

His case: he kept an open stance but made it narrower and opened the shoulder angle a little more. The reason was not vision. His old setup coiled so far that gathering force into one place took too long. The old form's big strength was that his front shoulder stayed completely closed all the way through. That was the single biggest reason it never flew open. Once he relaxed that, the shoulder sometimes came open, which had never been seen from him before.

So whatever stance you use, the front shoulder has to stay closed through landing and into the turn:
- Balance before force (Max Muncy). Take a short stride, gather energy behind you, then push onto your center. Keep the shoulders closed until the rotational force reaches the upper body, so stored energy is not leaked early. Keep the head still.
- Closed-Net Drill (Episode 1). A net about three feet behind you makes a tight space that helps keep the front shoulder from opening up.

If the open stance keeps leaking your front shoulder, the Lee example says it may cost you more than the power it adds.

Q. How do I spray the ball like a sprinkler instead of yanking everything pull side?

A. Build your approach on opposite-field hitting, the way Luis Arraez does. His nickname is La Regadera, "the sprinkler," because he can hit it anywhere.

Most hitters pull most of their balls because that is the direction of rotation and where force is easiest to make. Arraez, a left-handed hitter, does the opposite. His balls went about 38.9% to center, 32.6% to left (opposite field), and 28.5% to right. He works left-center and center on purpose. That is why he handles fastballs, breaking balls, and off-speed pitches.

How his swing makes it possible:
- Restraint. From stance to power position his hands barely move. The front leg going forward makes the energy. He builds almost no upper-body coil. The force comes from the weight shift.
- A short swing. His back elbow travels a short distance. Think of a boxing jab, not a big straight punch.
- Let the ball do the work. He puts the bat on the ball with only enough strength not to get overpowered. Balls die after clearing the infield, and his bat control threads balls down the lines.

The payoff is contact: about a 7.8% strikeout rate, about 95% contact on pitches in the zone, and about 89.5% contact on pitches outside it.

To practice going the other way, use the Angle Toss from the Off-Speed Pitch Approach note. For a right-handed hitter, the feeder tosses from the third-base side, so you have to let the ball travel and drive it to the opposite field.

Q. If I practice hitting inside pitches the opposite side of pull side then what is the benefits?

A. The benefit is that the pitcher runs out of places to throw.

The usual teaching on inside pitches is to shorten the arms and pull it with a body turn. Luis Arraez trains the opposite. He practices taking inside pitches the other way and never just rides the rotation.

What it gets you:
1. A hit instead of a rollover. When a pitcher challenges him inside, the result is a hit rather than a weak pull-side grounder.
2. No safe spot for the pitcher. Against a hitter who goes the other way, coming inside is the obvious counter. If you can hit that pitch the other way too, the pitcher has nowhere left to go.
3. A bigger hitting window for off-speed. Once you can handle the inside fastball deep and to the opposite field, you no longer cheat forward to protect against it. Then off-speed pitches become chances: caught deep goes opposite field, caught off the front foot goes up the middle, caught early goes pull side.

Be honest about the difficulty. Most left-handed hitters trying this on an inside pitch struggle to get the ball past the shortstop. Train it with the Angle Toss. For a right-handed hitter, the feeder tosses from the third-base side, which forces you to let the ball travel, stay inside it, and drive it the other way.

Q. If I am not a big guy, should I let go of the top hand after contact to add whip without losing contact?

A. It can work. Masataka Yoshida does it. The key is that the release happens after contact and your front hand is in control.

Yoshida is short with short arms. At contact his arms extend toward center field, and he lets go of the top hand almost every time. The reason is to make up for size. Releasing the top hand creates a longer swing arc, more centrifugal force, and a harder-hit ball. That offsets what he gives up in flexibility and raw strength. Yang Joon-hyuk's "banzai" finish works on the same idea.

It does not cost him contact because bat control lives in the front hand. His front-hand control is the best part of his swing. If your front hand cannot steer the barrel through the ball on its own, letting go of the top hand will hurt your contact instead of helping it.

Q. How do I keep a short takeaway and still drive the ball toward center?

A. Follow Masataka Yoshida's model: compact going back, full extension coming through.

1. Short takeback. Take the bat back only a little, then come forward without changing the path of your arms. That makes a clean in-and-out route.
2. Let the torso drive it. A torso-driven swing magnifies that clean path. Core strength is what lets a hitter with short arms swing very compactly and still hit hard.
3. A slight upward path. Use the mild upward barrel path of a modern barrel hitter, nothing extreme.
4. Extend toward center. At contact, let your arms extend in a follow-through that drives the ball back up the middle rather than pulling it. Yoshida also releases his top hand after contact for a longer arc. That only works with strong front-hand control.

Q. What is the money gap, and how does palm-up / palm-down stop me from rolling over too soon?

A. The money gap is the space between your forearms at contact and early in the follow-through. It is a checkpoint that shows you are squaring the ball and sending it in a good direction.

At contact you want:
- Top hand palm up.
- Bottom hand palm down.
- Arms connected to the body.
- Space between the forearms.
- The barrel moving through the ball's intended direction.

How palm-up, palm-down stops an early rollover: a rollover happens when the top hand turns over too soon. The barrel then cuts across the ball, which makes weak pull-side grounders or sharply hooked fouls. Keeping the top palm up and the bottom palm down at contact delays that turnover until the barrel has gone through the ball. That leads to more hard-hit balls and a better launch angle.

Do not hold the position after contact. The forearms turn over naturally once the barrel is through. The money gap is a checkpoint, not a pose. Train it with the top-hand and bottom-hand drills from the same episode.

Q. How do the top-hand and bottom-hand drills teach me to send the ball the other way instead of hooking it foul?

A. Both drills teach you that the body sets the ball's direction and the hand only finishes it. When the arms pull on their own or the top hand rolls early, the barrel cuts across toward the pull side and hooks the ball foul. Directions below are for a right-handed hitter. Lefties reverse them.

Top-Hand Drill. Think of a third baseman turning a double play: the body starts it, the shoulder stays connected, the hand finishes.
1. Hold a baseball or soft ball in your top hand. Take an athletic stance facing a net or partner. No bat at first.
2. Load the rear shoulder blade without pulling the elbow far behind you.
3. Keep the top hand near the rear shoulder.
4. Rotate with the hips and torso.
5. Let the arm move into a compact throwing position.
6. Throw toward your target and finish with a controlled release.
Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mH0x1aptzog) at 1:39. In the swing, this keeps the top palm up at contact and stops early pronation, the rollover behind weak pull-side grounders.

Bottom-Hand Drill:
1. Hold a ball in your bottom hand, close to your body, arm connected to the torso. Pick a safe target such as a net.
2. Load into the rear hip.
3. Move forward and land balanced.
4. Rotate with the lower body and torso.
5. Let the back shoulder work toward the target.
6. Keep the bottom hand connected. Do not reach with the arm.
7. Release in the same direction your body is turning.
Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mH0x1aptzog) at 3:01.

Why the ball goes where you aim: your release follows your rotation. To send it to center, land balanced, turn the back shoulder through center, and release toward the middle. Aim right-center to go the other way.

Avoid: rolling the top hand before contact, pulling with only the arms, pushing the bottom hand away from the body, letting the hands go before the hips turn, and spinning so hard that the barrel cuts to the pull side.

Q. I struck out looking early. Should I still die with my plan instead of guessing next AB?

A. Yes. Stick with your plan.

Have a specific plan for every at-bat and stay with it. If you are going to fail, fail while executing your plan, not while guessing randomly. That was the core logic for David Ortiz and Manny Ramirez.

Baseball is a game of failure. Failing seven times out of ten still makes you great. Ortiz called Manny a "genius" because he did not care if he struck out early. He knew he would get that pitcher later in the game, or the next day.

Ortiz also says the analytics are only about 85% of it. The other 15% is mentality, what he calls the "Mamba mentality": handling adversity and staying hungry. One looking strikeout should not make you drop your plan.

Q. Can I look lost on a pitch early so I can crush that same pitch later in the game?

A. Yes. Manny Ramirez did exactly that.

He used early at-bats to set pitchers up. He might take a strikeout or look "lost" on a certain pitch to build the pitcher's confidence in that sequence. Later in the game, when the pitcher went back to it, he "burned" them by hitting that same pitch "into the highway."

It works only because he had a specific plan and stuck to it. He did not care about striking out early, because he knew he would get the pitcher later. If you try it, go in with a clear plan for which pitch you are setting up. Do not just give away at-bats.

Q. Why am I early on every changeup even when I try to sit off-speed?

A. You probably do not have an off-speed problem. You have a fastball problem.

Many hitters are taught to attack the inside fastball by getting the bat head out in front. Over time that becomes their only contact point, and every fastball gets pulled. The fastball is the fastest pitch you will see. If your timing is already committed out front for it, there is nowhere left to go when something slower comes. The changeup arrives after your swing is spent, even when you are trying to sit on it.

The fix is to build the biggest hitting window. Take away the pitcher's best weapon, the inside fastball, by letting it travel and driving it to the opposite field. Once you can do that, you stop cheating forward. Then the changeup becomes a chance:
- Caught deep: drive it to the opposite field.
- Caught off the front foot: drive it up the middle.
- Caught early: drive it to the pull side.
Every timing has a good result.

Drills, in this order:
1. Angle Toss. For a right-handed hitter, the feeder tosses from the third-base side. You have to let the ball travel and drive it the other way.
2. BOSU Ball Drill. Get down to the pitch with your legs, the inside of the back knee driving down, instead of reaching with your hands. Keep the bat shaft connected to your back shoulder.
3. V Drill. Lead arm holds the bat. The top hand opens into a V, palm up, off the handle. The back shoulder has to keep rotating, which stops the rollover and creates backspin when you are out front.

Avoid pulling every fastball, reaching with your hands for off-speed, and letting the hands finish the swing when the back shoulder stops.

Q. How do I hit heaters without cheating out front so hanging breakers are still hittable?

A. Let the fastball travel, especially the inside one, and learn to drive it to the opposite field. Then you never have to cheat out front.

If you pull every fastball, your one contact point sits out in front of the plate. There is nothing left for a slower pitch. Once you can handle the inside fastball deep and to the opposite field, the worst case on that pitch is a ball dropped into the opposite field. You stop protecting against it, and your swing has room left for off-speed.

The contact-point map you want:
- Late or letting it travel: deep, back of the plate, to the opposite field.
- On time: off the front foot, to the middle.
- Early: in front of the plate, to the pull side.
You do not pick the destination ahead of time. You build a swing that gives a good result at every point.

With hanging breakers, a hanging breaking ball in the middle of the zone does not need a special move. Just stay ready for it. If you are early on a breaker, keep the back shoulder turning with the top palm up (the V Drill). You get backspin, which turns an early swing into a line drive over the shortstop, a gap double, or a pull-side home run instead of a grounder to third.

Drill order: Angle Toss (direction first), then BOSU Ball Drill (lower half), then V Drill (upper half and finish).

Q. If I time every at-bat to the fastball, where should my hands start?

A. Near your chest. Corey Seager times every plate appearance to the fastball. He sets his hands near the chest, where they can be drawn fastest and where a human naturally produces force.

The rest of his setup supports it. He uses a small front-foot tap as a trigger instead of a high leg lift. His stride is aggressive while his weight stays back. The barrel comes around wide instead of stabbing at the ball, so the bat face can match the roughly 5-degree angle most pitches have at the front of the plate.

Q. How do I stride hard without leaking my weight onto the front side too soon?

A. Copy Corey Seager's pattern. Use a small trigger, stride far, and keep the weight back.

1. Trigger with a tap. Start the swing with a tap of the front foot, not a high leg lift.
2. Ordinary width, aggressive stride. His stance and stride width are normal. The stride itself is aggressive, and the front leg goes far forward.
3. Do not let the weight follow the foot. The front leg strides out but the weight never leaks with it. That is what holds the power position.
4. Unwind in order. From that power position the coiled body unwinds knee, then pelvis, then torso, then shoulders.

Q. Can I still hit for power with a flatter path if I keep weight back and take a long stride?

A. Yes. Giancarlo Stanton does exactly that. But his swing runs on rare raw strength, and it costs a lot of strikeouts.

His pattern:
1. Closed stance with a front-foot timing tap and a toe step.
2. A very good hip hinge at setup. After the stride, his arms are still where they started.
3. A small coil. He has noticeably less upper-lower separation than other home-run hitters.
4. A long stride that makes up for it. The front foot travels a long way, which thickens the coil instead of sharpening it.
5. A flat path, not an uppercut. It is a slight, smooth upward path. His shoulders do not drop as low as Aaron Judge's or Mike Trout's.
6. Weight stays back, up to about 60% behind, even with that wide stride. The front leg brakes firmly and the back foot scissors slightly, which adds force.

The image is a heavy polearm, not a blade: thick and solid rather than quick. It works because of his size, about 2 meters and over 110 kg, and his strength. His average exit velocity is around 93 mph, in the top 1% every year since 2015. The cost is a high strikeout rate and a low contact rate.

Q. If my exit velo is huge, why do I still strike out so much on a max-effort swing?

A. Because the swing that produces that exit velocity does not change with two strikes.

Giancarlo Stanton averages around 93 mph off the bat. He has been in the top 1% every year since tracking began in 2015. Exit velocity matters, because hit and home-run chances rise with it. But he takes the same max-effort swing in every count. A high strikeout rate and a low contact rate are the necessary result. This season he struck out 28% of the time, versus about 22% for Pete Alonso and 24% for Max Muncy.

It has worked for him because a career on-base percentage around .353 and steady home runs carry the strikeouts. When he barrels the ball, it is devastating.

Another factor is elasticity. Compared with 2017, his 2022 swing looks stiffer, whether from age or heavy lifting. That stiffness likely makes adjusting harder and his reactions later. More strikeouts follow.

Q. If I lengthen my stride to hit breaking balls, will I get late on heaters?

A. That is what happened to Gunnar Henderson. The longer stride fixed breaking balls and opened a hole against fastballs.

He widened his stride and weight transfer by about one foot's width. Nothing else changed. The bigger weight shift gave him a sturdier base, a wider timing window, and a visibly stronger swing.

Against breaking balls, he went from a .237 average, .479 slugging, and .330 wOBA to .333, .870, and .537. Against off-speed, from .241, .483, and .307 to .286, .612, and .573.

The cost: timing set for spin arrives slightly late on fastballs, especially in unfavorable counts. Against fastballs his average fell from .250 to .194, his whiff rate rose from 27.2% to 29.1%, and his strikeout rate rose from 26% to 32.1%.

His overall 24% strikeout rate is not the issue. Aaron Judge is at 24.4%. Getting beaten by the pitch he sees most often is.

Q. How do I keep a bigger weight shift without getting beaten by fastballs in 0-2?

A. The Henderson note describes this problem but does not give a fix.

The problem: Gunnar Henderson widened his stride and weight shift by about one foot's width. That improved his breaking-ball and off-speed numbers and his power. But his timing is now set for spin. He arrives slightly late on fastballs, especially in unfavorable counts like 0-2, which is exactly where his breaking-ball work improved most. His fastball average fell from .250 to .194 and his strikeout rate on fastballs rose from 26% to 32.1%.

The closest answer in the notes is Seiya Suzuki's adjustment against major-league velocity. He moved his contact point earlier, shortened his stride, and started his hands earlier to buy a step of preparation. The shorter stride costs some coil and weight transfer. That is the trade you would make in two-strike counts.

Q. Can I still get on base if I hunt pull-side homers almost every swing?

A. Yes, if you walk a lot. That is how Kyle Schwarber does it.

He batted .201 but had a .349 on-base percentage, a gap of about 148 points. He had 106 hits (43 home runs) and 116 walks, more walks than hits.

His approach has three deliberate choices:
1. Pull. He sets his contact point about one ball's width in front of normal. About 60% of his balls are pulled and only about 17% go the other way.
2. Guess. He has huge strength but limited bat control, so he picks a location and pitch type before the at-bat and times everything to the fastball.
3. Swing aggressively. If it is his pitch, he swings from the first pitch in any count, especially at high and up-and-in pitches.

The walks come from two things. He owns a zone and ignores pitches outside it. Pitchers fear his power, so they nibble at the edges and miss.

The cost: he is weak against breaking balls (especially sliders that tunnel off the fastball) and off-speed. His whiff rate is in the bottom 17% of the league, and he strikes out a lot. It is mostly walk, strikeout, or home run. Without strict zone discipline and real power, this approach does not get you on base.

Q. How do I own a personal zone so I walk a lot even when my average is low?

A. Decide on your zone before the pitch and refuse everything outside it, the way Kyle Schwarber does.

1. Own a zone. If a pitch is not in the zone he has set for himself, he has no interest in it. He is aggressive and patient at the same time. He will even take a strike in the zone without flinching if it is not the pitch he came to hit. Only with two strikes does he loosen that rule.
2. Make the pitcher pay for mistakes. Pitchers know that if they miss over the plate, the ball leaves the park. So they work the edges, miss outside, and walk him. His walks are partly earned and partly given.

The result: a .201 average but a .349 on-base percentage, with 116 walks against 106 hits. The second part depends on real power. Pitchers only nibble at hitters they fear.

Q. If my batting average is not high, can pitch selection still make my power play?

A. Yes. That is Max Muncy's whole case.

Hitting for power needs two skills. One is contact. The other is pitch judgment: knowing which pitch you can punish and waiting for it, like a sniper. A hitter with real power needs only one of the two to survive. Hitters with both, like Aaron Judge, Mike Trout, and Yordan Alvarez, run the league.

Muncy survives on judgment. He is a career .231 hitter, around .250 even in his best years. But his eight-season on-base percentage is .357, a gap of about 125 points over his average. This season it is .254 with a .411 on-base percentage. For comparison, Judge's and Trout's gaps are about 110 points and Ohtani's about 91.

That patience sits on top of real power: 35 home runs in 2018, 35 in 2019, 12 in shortened 2020, 36 in 2021, and 21 in 2022 while playing hurt. Waiting for your pitch lets your power play even when your average is low.

Q. How do I keep my head still and front shoulder closed while I transfer weight?

A. Use Max Muncy's rule: balance before force.

1. Take a short stride.
2. Gather your energy behind you.
3. Push forward onto your center axis.
4. Keep the shoulders closed until the rotational force reaches the upper body. If the front shoulder opens early, the energy you stored leaks out before you can use it.
5. Keep the head still the whole time.

The head is the non-negotiable part. If the head moves, mechanics are no longer the conversation. The swing has to be rebuilt from scratch.

Q. I already hit the ball hard. Should I tilt more upper swing just to chase home runs?

A. It is a trade, not a free upgrade. Mike Trout made this exact move.

For most of his career Trout swung close to level. He turned his strength into hard contact and waited for his zone and his pitch. That made him a high-OPS, gap-to-gap hitter rather than a pure slugger.

In 2022 he tilted the swing upward into a home-run path. Ground balls and line drives dropped, fly balls rose, he swung more often, and he still hit the ball harder. It was his best home-run rate per plate appearance ever.

The cost was contact: more whiffs, more strikeouts, fewer walks, and real ups and downs from game to game for a hitter who used to have almost none. If you already hit the ball hard, a more upward path can add home runs. Decide whether you can give up that contact and those walks.

Q. If I swing more for fly balls, what do I give up in contact and walks?

A. Based on Mike Trout's 2022 change, you give up contact, walks, and consistency.

When he tilted his path upward for more fly balls, he hit more home runs per plate appearance than ever. But he also had:
- More swings and misses.
- More strikeouts.
- Fewer walks.
- Fewer ground balls and line drives.
- More game-to-game swings in results, from a hitter who used to have almost none.

He also swung more often overall. That added home runs but cut into the patient approach that had made him a high-OPS hitter.

Q. Fastballs keep beating me. Do I just move my contact point earlier?

A. Moving your contact point earlier is part of it. Seiya Suzuki did exactly that against major-league velocity, but he changed his whole timing, not just the contact spot.

His rookie year in Chicago: a .260s average, 14 home runs, a 24% strikeout rate, and an 89.6 mph average exit velocity, middle of the league. He found MLB velocity harder than expected.

What he changed:
1. Contact point. He named this himself. He pulled his timing a beat or two earlier.
2. Shorter stride. It costs some coil and some weight transfer but buys a step of preparation.
3. Earlier hands. His hands started close to a full tempo sooner on video.
Nothing else needed touching. He also got healthy and built a routine that fit him.

One caution: earlier timing is not the same as reaching far out front. Reaching way out front, especially on inside pitches, hooks balls foul and leaves you exposed to changeups away. Keep the front-foot contact window and get ready sooner.

Q. Is a shorter stride worth it if it gets me ready one beat sooner?

A. For Seiya Suzuki, it was. After a rookie year where major-league velocity beat him (24% strikeout rate, 14 home runs), he shortened his stride along with moving his contact point earlier.

The trade is clear. A shorter stride costs some coil and some weight transfer. In return it buys a step of preparation. He paired it with starting his hands earlier, close to a full tempo sooner on video. Nothing else in his mechanics changed, because they were never the problem.

It is worth it if being late on fastballs is your real problem. You give up a little stored power to be ready on time.

Q. How do I get power from the front knee without loading my hands extra?

A. Follow Shohei Ohtani's minimalist swing. Keep the hands quiet and let the legs make the power.

1. Simple start. From the stance, when the ball comes, the swing just goes.
2. Toe step. Plant the front leg forward. The hands stretch only as far as the front leg moves. Nothing pulls farther back and nothing drops.
3. Power from the front knee. The pelvis folds, both legs settle slightly toward the ground, and the hips push a little forward. That builds the power position.
4. Rotation. The front knee, which had come in, turns outward to create rotational force. The back knee turns the same way at the same time, and the pelvis turns with them. That sends force to the point of contact.
5. Arms. The elbow tucks in tight, and the angle between the hands on the bat and the forearm is kept.

Q. Why keep the rear-elbow angle instead of fully extending through contact?

A. Holding that angle is what keeps the power in the swing. In Shohei Ohtani's swing, the elbow tucks in as tight as possible, but the angle between the hands on the bat and the forearm stays the same. American coaching treats holding that angle through the finish as essential for producing power. Hitters are not taught to straighten the arms.

The bat-path note explains why. The power comes from the body turning while the barrel lags behind and then accelerates through the pitch, without an early, isolated wrist flick. If you fully straighten out early, you release that angle before the barrel gets through the ball, and the force leaks out.

Q. If I am fast, does raising my walk rate matter more than rebuilding my swing?

A. If your swing is already sound, getting on base more is what turns your speed into value. That was Elly De La Cruz's leap.

His speed is elite. His 30.5 ft/sec sprint speed led MLB, and he covered 90 feet in 3.67 seconds. As a rookie he hit .235 with a .710 OPS and an 84 wRC+, below average, even though his swing was already stable.

The real improvement in year two was plate discipline. His walk rate rose from 8.2% to 14.4%, about six points higher, and his on-base and slugging both went up. Through 29 games he was hitting .280 with a .980 OPS and 18 steals. A higher on-base percentage turns his best tool loose. At .280 with a .390-.410 on-base percentage, people started talking about Rickey Henderson's 130-steal record.

The note does not say to never work on your swing. It shows that for a fast player, more times on base pays off quickly.

Q. Should I drop my weak side as a switch hitter to protect that speed edge?

A. The notes lean yes when the gap is as big as Elly De La Cruz's. They also warn it is hard to do.

His split as a rookie (2023): left-handed against righties, 70 hits in 305 at-bats, 11 home runs, a .799 OPS. Right-handed, 21 hits in 114 at-bats, 2 home runs, a .184 average, a .495 OPS. It held in 2024: .311 with a 1.105 OPS from the left, .229 with a .729 OPS from the right.

Dropping the right side would help his hitting and protect his speed. Batting left starts you a step closer to first base. Among the fastest home-to-first runners, only Bobby Witt Jr. batted right-handed. But switch hitters rarely find it easy to give up a side.

Q. Why would I leave an open stance that already made me mash?

A. Leave it only if it is causing a specific problem. Aaron Judge did not switch because square is "better."

He hit 62 home runs and, as a rookie, 52 from an open stance. He struggled in the World Series while already square. Open versus square is not the real question.

His reasons: you return to square to swing anyway. But starting about 20 degrees open meant that even after his leg lift he sometimes did not feel square, as if he was not facing the pitcher. Outside pitches seemed far away. Starting closer to square, about 5 degrees open, removed that feeling and improved his coverage away.

The deeper reason was how pitchers attacked him. Nobody wants to pitch Judge inside, so he saw a steady diet of pitches away, breaking balls off the outer edge, and more sweepers. He had a weakness, and he moved to close it. It worked: he came out of his 2024 slump, hit 58 home runs, and won the AL home-run title and MVP.

So keep the open stance unless pitchers are beating you somewhere it leaves exposed.

Q. Everyone throws me away and sweepers. Should I start more square so outside pitches feel closer?

A. That is exactly why Aaron Judge went square.

Pitchers would not work him inside, so he got a steady diet of pitches away and breaking balls off the outer edge. The league's rise in sweepers made it worse. From his old stance, about 20 degrees open, he sometimes did not feel square even after his leg lift, and outside pitches felt far away. Starting closer to square, about 5 degrees open, removed that feeling and improved his coverage away.

It paid off: he came out of his 2024 slump with 58 home runs, the AL home-run title, and MVP.

Make the change for that reason, not because square is better in general. Judge also hit 62 home runs from the open stance.

Q. Should I cut my swing rate and hunt one pitch per at-bat instead of attacking everything?

A. For Pete Crow-Armstrong, it turned his career around.

Pitchers attacked his free swinging with spin. Over about 70 games he hit .195 with a .533 OPS and a 47 wRC+, last among 149 qualified hitters. Alex Bregman's advice was simple: swing less. Stop attacking everything and hunt one pitch per at-bat.

The changes:
- Swing rate: 60% down to 49%.
- First-pitch swings: 53% down to 39%.
- Chase rate: 41.7% down to 34.1%.
- Walk rate: 4.5% up to 12%.

His home runs now come on timing he predicted instead of ambushes out in front. Along with a stance change and added strength, he hit .287 with a .388 on-base percentage, .545 slugging, 36 home runs, 30 steals, and a 156 wRC+ through 118 games.

Q. Will standing a few inches closer to the plate help me against lefty stuff away?

A. It did for Pete Crow-Armstrong. Anthony Rizzo told him to stand about 7 cm (about 3 inches) closer to the plate.

The closer spot made outside pitches from left-handers easier to read. It also sped up his reaction to inside pitches. Against left-handed pitching he went from a .188 average and .593 OPS to .315 and .940.

He made this change along with hunting one pitch per at-bat and adding strength, so it was not the only reason his numbers improved.

Q. Can I hit for power without sitting on my back leg like a classic slugger?

A. Yes. Ronald Acuña Jr. is the proof.

He does not sit on the back leg the way most home-run hitters do. He starts near a 50/50 balance and moves his weight forward. His elbow never opens the wide rear space power hitters use to build force. Still, in 2023 he hit 41 home runs with 73 steals, a .337 average, a 1.012 OPS, and a 170 wRC+. He is the only 40-70 player ever.

The kinetic-chain notes describe two ways to deliver energy. One is an active forward weight shift (like Yordan Alvarez). The other is rotating on the back-leg axis (like Bryce Harper, Aaron Judge, Shohei Ohtani). Both produce power.

The catch: Acuña's version works because it is paired with his eye, his restraint, and his elasticity. His shallow shoulder entry also tends to produce lower launch angles and line drives rather than high fly balls.

Q. Why would a compact down-swing still work if my eyes and timing are good?

A. Because the swing's job is square, well-timed contact, and a compact path can deliver that when the eye and timing are elite.

Ronald Acuña Jr.'s swing stabs in compactly, in the down-swing lineage rather than the uppercut now standard in MLB. On paper that looks dated. Combined with his eye, his restraint, and his elasticity, it works: a .337 average, 41 home runs, and a 170 wRC+.

Two things keep it productive:
1. The money gap. Top palm up, bottom palm down at contact delays the rollover. That keeps the barrel from cutting across the ball and supports a high hard-hit rate and a good launch angle.
2. The "down swing" is often a feeling. Many big leaguers describe swinging down, but the barrel still travels a circular, slightly upward path. The feeling comes from the elbow driving down toward the body. A shallow shoulder entry like Acuña's leads to lower launch angles and line drives.

A caution: a truly downward barrel path (negative attack angle) crosses the pitch for only an instant. It produces topped grounders and rollovers and leaves little room for timing errors. Keep the compact feel, but the barrel still needs to get on plane.
