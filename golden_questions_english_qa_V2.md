Q1. How do I hit the outside pitch?

A. Do not let the outside pitch travel deep. The old three-ball teaching (inside pitch far out front, middle pitch at the plate, outside pitch deep) is outdated. Pro hitters meet every pitch in about the same contact window: near the plane of the front foot after it lands. On the outside pitch you change the barrel's direction, not your contact depth. A right-handed hitter drives it toward right-center.

Why not deep: against 95-98 mph there is very little reaction time. Waiting until the ball reaches the catcher's side of the plate makes the barrel late. You get swings and misses, fouls straight back, weak opposite-field flies, or contact off the handle.

How to cover it:
1. Land in a balanced position.
2. Keep the hands connected to the body.
3. Let the back shoulder work toward the pitch.
4. Use the legs, back knee, and back hip to rotate.
5. Turn the barrel behind the ball. Use a south-to-north (Ferris-wheel) path, not an east-to-west swing around the body.
6. Keep going through the outer part of the plate.
7. Drive the ball to the middle or the opposite-field gap.

Drill: the BOSU Ball Drill. Put a BOSU trainer between your legs as a guide (do not stand on it). Set a tee low and outside. Load into the back hip, land, drive the back knee and back hip toward the hitting area, let the back shoulder work toward the pitch, and turn the barrel behind the ball toward the middle or opposite field. For a reference, watch this video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bMPY5FJup6g) at 2:47. Then move to front toss: start low and away, then middle-away and high-away, and adjust the barrel angle to each height.

Avoid reaching with the arms, pulling the front shoulder away, cutting across the ball, and losing balance. Cue: "One contact window, three barrel directions."

Q2. How do I hit a curveball?

A. Curveballs are hard for three reasons. They move below or away from where your eyes first expect them. They arrive slower than the fastball, so early hands make you swing early, reach, roll over, or pull off. And the breaking ball is often the pitcher's best weapon.

Four mechanics fix most of it:
1. Match the barrel to the pitch's path. A downward-breaking pitch needs a stronger hip hinge, more shoulder tilt, a lower barrel entry, and a controlled low-to-high path through contact. This is not a big uppercut. Follow the ball with your eyes while the head stays stable. Do not collapse the whole body toward a low pitch.
2. Stay compact without pushing the hands. Be short into the zone and long through it. Do not chop down at a breaking ball.
3. Keep your body direction. Do not pull the front shoulder away from a ball breaking toward the outer edge. Let the back shoulder work toward the pitch.
4. "The way you come in is the way you go out." A barrel that enters behind the ball on the pitch's plane stays in the zone longer.

Separation buys you the time. Let the lower body stride forward while the upper body stays loaded, and land with the hands still near the rear shoulder. Because the hands have not fired yet, you can delay the final barrel turn when the slower pitch comes. Start the turn with a "roll" of the rear elbow and barrel, not a hand push. A push makes a steep "V" path. A roll makes a long "Nike swoosh" path.

Drills:
- The Roll Drill: hold a basketball near the rear shoulder, stride while keeping it there, land without committing the hands, then rotate and let the rear elbow and barrel work into the slot. Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xjtune7wX5s) at 2:10. Move from dry swings to tee to front toss.
- Ferris-Wheel and Closed-Net drills (Episodes 4 and 1) for barrel plane and compactness.
- Recognition progression: watch breaking balls without swinging, call the pitch early, then alternate fastballs and breaking balls in front toss. Swing only at ones that finish in your zone. Take the ones that finish below it.

Avoid committing the hands before you recognize the pitch, chopping down, letting the head drift, and trying to lift every breaking ball.

Q3. What is launch angle and what should mine be?

A. Launch angle is the vertical direction of the ball right after contact, measured in degrees. It describes the ball, not the bat. The bat's direction before contact is a different number called attack angle.

General ranges:
- Below 0 degrees: driven into the ground.
- 0-10 degrees: ground ball or low line drive.
- 10-20 degrees: line drive.
- 20-30 degrees: strong fly ball or home run, if exit velocity (ball speed off the bat, in mph) is high enough.
- Very high angles: easy fly balls or pop-ups.

What should yours be? There is no single magic number. Launch angle only means something next to exit velocity, contact quality, and direction. A ball hit about 100 mph at about 25 degrees has strong home-run potential. Most hitters are not near 100 mph. For many hitters, steady hard line drives around 10-20 degrees (about 15 degrees) are a better goal than trying to lift every ball to 20-25 degrees. The notes do not give a separate high school target.

How to get it: launch angle should be the result of a good swing, not something you force. Load into the rear hip, land balanced, turn the barrel behind the ball, and get on plane early. Getting on plane means matching the barrel to the pitch's slightly downward path before contact, then keeping it there through the zone. Hit near the center of the ball and keep going toward your target.

Avoid scooping or leaning back to make lift. Also avoid chopping down ("get on top of the ball"). A steep downswing gives the bat and ball only a short moment together. It produces chopped grounders, rollovers, and pop-ups.

Q4. What is attack angle and how is it different from launch angle?

A. Attack angle is the angle of the barrel's path right before contact, in degrees. Launch angle is the angle of the ball right after contact. One describes the bat. The other describes the ball.

- Attack angle: direction of the bat, before and during contact.
- Launch angle: direction of the batted ball, right after contact.
- Exit velocity: speed of the batted ball after contact.

Positive attack angle: the barrel moves forward and slightly upward into contact. It is a smooth, shallow rise, not a big uppercut. It matches the downward path of the pitch, keeps the barrel in the zone longer, and gives more room for solid contact. It produces hard line drives and fly balls from gap to gap.

Negative attack angle: the barrel moves downward into the ball ("swing down," "chop"). It crosses the pitch's path instead of riding with it, like two roads meeting at an intersection. Results are topped grounders, weak contact, rollovers, pop-ups if you cut under the ball, and a tiny margin for timing errors.

The two numbers are linked but not the same. With the same positive attack angle, launch angle depends on where the barrel meets the ball:
- Above center: ground ball.
- Center: hard line drive.
- Slightly below center: line drive with lift.
- Too far below center: pop-up.

So a positive attack angle is only half of it. You still have to square the ball. Do not chase launch angle by dropping the back shoulder, lowering the hands, leaning back, or scooping. The notes give no target number for attack angle; they describe a small, positive angle.

To improve it: start balanced, load into the back hip without dropping the barrel, land in an adjustable position, turn the barrel onto the pitch's plane, keep it through the zone, and square the ball.

Q5. How should I load my hips before the swing?

A. The hip load (also called the hip hinge, the gather, or getting into an athletic position) puts your weight and tension into the rear hip before the swing. It gives you two things: balance, so you can hold still for a moment while you track the pitch, and power, because the rear glute and hamstring are ready to rotate the body.

Hinge at the hip. Do not only bend the knees.
1. Start in a comfortable stance.
2. Shift your balance into the rear leg.
3. Bend both knees slightly.
4. Move the hips back, like closing a car door with your rear hip. Think of pulling the rear hip toward the dugout behind you.
5. Keep your back strong and neutral. Do not round the lower back.
6. Hold it and feel the rear glute and hamstring turn on.
7. Stay balanced, with your weight inside the rear foot.

It looks like the start of a Romanian deadlift (RDL). The chest may tilt forward a little, like Mike Trout or Joey Votto. Do not go too deep.

Two mistakes to avoid:
- Sway: the whole body drifts over or past the rear foot. The head moves back, weight goes to the outside of the rear foot, and you have to rush forward to recover.
- Quad-dominant: hips stay tall, chest stays upright, the rear knee drives forward over the toes, and you feel nothing in the glute or hamstring.

Q6. How do I separate my hands during the swing?

A. Separate with a "scap load," not by pushing your hands away from your body.

Separation means the lower body strides toward the pitcher while the upper body stays loaded. The wrong way is to "walk away from your hands": the hands drift back or out, the shaft of the bat gets far from the body, and you lose barrel consistency.

The right way:
1. Get into your back hip as you stride.
2. Load the rear shoulder blade (scapula) slightly toward the spine. The hands move back with it.
3. When the front foot lands (the 50-50 position), keep the bat shaft close to the rear shoulder, with the knob pointing toward the catcher.
4. Stay square to the plate. Do not dump the front shoulder in.
5. Keep the rear elbow up. Do not let it drop.

Quick check for a coach or parent: from a front view at landing, you should see the rear elbow behind the hitter's back. If you cannot see it, the hitter is not separating correctly.

Why it matters: Mike Trout and Mookie Betts keep the bat close to their center of mass. Their swings are more consistent, and they handle both fastballs and off-speed pitches better. Ronald Acuña Jr. gets his hands farther from his body for bat speed, and he goes through more slumps.

Practice it with the Roll Drill: hold a basketball near the rear shoulder, stride while it stays there, land without committing the hands, then rotate. Do not over-stretch the hands behind you. That creates tension and disconnects the arms from the torso.

Q7. How do I hit a high pitch?

A. Keep the same swing and change its angle. A low pitch needs more shoulder tilt and a more upward barrel path. A high pitch needs less shoulder tilt, a flatter back shoulder, and a flatter barrel path that turns straight through the height of the ball. Think of the barrel as an extension of your shoulders.

How: make a controlled move forward and land balanced. Start the turn with the legs and hips. Let the back shoulder work toward and through the pitch, with the torso and barrel connected. Do not reach up with the hands or stand up out of your stance. Keep your hip hinge.

What the Ferris wheel is: many hitters swing flat around the body, an east-to-west or "merry-go-round" swing. That barrel cuts across the zone. For a right-handed hitter it races toward the left side, which makes pulled grounders and hooked fouls. The Ferris-wheel swing is a south-to-north path. The barrel comes from behind the body into the hitting zone, then moves forward through the ball with a controlled upward direction. Picture a Ferris wheel turning toward the pitcher instead of a merry-go-round spinning around you. It is not a steep uppercut. On a high pitch the same idea applies, with a flatter shoulder angle.

Drills:
- Drop-Bat Drill (Ferris wheel, credited to Craig Wallenbach): take your normal stance. On an easy front toss, let the barrel settle into its working position (do not let go of the bat). Turn the barrel into the pitch's path, from behind the ball, forward and slightly up through contact. Start slow. Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Z5r9275-yM) at 3:10.
- PVC Pipe Drill: put a light PVC pipe (about 2 inches wide) across your shoulders. Use tennis balls only. A partner tosses to different heights. Stay balanced with the legs and hip hinge, turn the torso, and point the back shoulder at the ball so the pipe travels through the ball's plane. Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AyXaOtquLck) at 1:52.
- Tee Drill: start the tee mid-zone and raise it step by step. Keep the top hand strong, keep barrel lag, match the back-shoulder angle to the ball's height, and drive it on a line.

Discipline: as a guide, do not chase above about belly-button height. Avoid a steep uppercut on high pitches, dropping the back shoulder, standing up, and lifting the hands on their own.

Q8. What is the Ferris Wheel drill?

A. In the notes, the Ferris wheel is a swing path, and the Drop-Bat Drill is how you train it.

The problem it fixes: many hitters swing flat around the body. This is the east-to-west or "merry-go-round" swing. The barrel cuts across the hitting zone. For a right-handed hitter it races toward the left side after contact, which causes pulled ground balls and sharply hooked fouls.

The Ferris-wheel (south-to-north) path: the barrel works from behind the body into the hitting zone, then forward through the ball with a controlled upward direction. A Ferris wheel turns toward the pitcher. A merry-go-round spins around you. It does not mean a steep uppercut. The barrel turns into the pitch's path and keeps moving forward.

The Drop-Bat Drill, step by step:
1. Take your normal batting stance.
2. A coach or partner gives an easy front toss.
3. As the ball is tossed, let the barrel settle into its working position while you keep control of the bat.
4. Turn the barrel into the pitch's path.
5. The barrel goes from behind the ball, forward through the zone, and slightly upward through contact.
6. Start slow. Speed up only once you can repeat it safely.

"Drop the bat" does not mean letting go of the bat. It means letting the barrel settle before you turn it through the zone. Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Z5r9275-yM) at 3:10. The drill is credited to hitting instructor Craig Wallenbach.

What it builds: the ball travels into a better contact area, you stop rushing the hands, fewer pulled grounders and hooked fouls, you can handle inside, middle, and outside pitches, and you stay balanced and square to the pitcher.

The Mike Trout link: in batting practice Trout drives his first round to right-center and right field, then works the middle, then a few to left-center. The lesson is not to copy his swing. It is to stay balanced, stay square, and build a barrel path that uses the whole field.

Q9. Why is top hand so important in batting?

A. The top hand is the upper hand on the bat: the right hand for a right-handed hitter, the left hand for a lefty. It is often the dominant hand. It turns the barrel onto the pitch's path and delivers it through the hitting zone. Used well, it gets the barrel into the zone early and keeps it there longer. That cuts down rollovers, pop-ups, fouls, and swings under breaking balls.

Two common mistakes:
1. Hammering down to "get on top" of the ball. The top forearm pronates (the palm turns down) too early. The barrel then cuts steeply across the pitch instead of riding through it. Results: topped grounders, weak rollovers to the pull side, hooked fouls, a short hitting zone, less carry, and trouble with the outside slider.
2. Disconnecting the hands. The hitter tries to make bat speed by pushing the top hand away from the rear shoulder. The arms work apart from the body and the barrel leaves its track. It feels fast but is not useful bat speed. Results: pop-ups, fouls, whiffs, contact off the end, and trouble adjusting to breaking balls and changeups.

The correct move: "Connect, roll, and deliver the pizza."
1. Connect: during the load and stride, keep the top hand near the rear shoulder. Connected means the hand, arm, shoulder, and torso move together. It does not mean pressed rigidly against the shoulder. At landing the hand is still back.
2. Roll: once you decide to swing, the lower body and torso turn. The rear elbow works down and around as the barrel turns behind you into the slot. This is not rolling the wrists over the ball.
3. Deliver the pizza: through contact, keep the top palm facing up, as if carrying a pizza (supination). At contact: top palm up, bottom palm down, hands connected, space between the forearms, barrel moving through the ball. After contact the hands turn over naturally. The goal is to delay the rollover, not to stop it.

How it shows up in hitting: you get on plane earlier, stay through contact longer, control the barrel on every location, adjust to off-speed, avoid early rollover, and drive the ball up the middle. Practice with the Roll Drill (Episode 9) and the Direction Drill (Episode 7).

Q10. Where should my contact point be?

A. Your contact point should be about the same for every pitch: a window near the plane of your front foot after it lands. Picture a vertical window that starts at the front foot. That is your one reliable reference.

The old way had three depths: inside pitch far out front, middle pitch at the plate, outside pitch deep. That is outdated. Reaching far out front on an inside pitch makes the barrel turn sharply and hook the ball foul. It also makes you cheat for the inside fastball, so a changeup away beats you. Letting a 95-98 mph outside pitch get too deep makes you late.

What changes by location is the barrel's direction, not the depth:
- Inside: turn tighter, hands close, drive it to the pull-side gap (left-center for a righty).
- Middle: drive it through center field.
- Outside: let the barrel work to the opposite field (right-center for a righty).

Cue: "One contact window, three barrel directions."

Steps: land before the final barrel turn, set the front-foot contact plane, adjust the barrel's direction, and keep going through the ball after contact.

This does not mean falling onto your front leg. It describes where the ball is relative to your body. Moving up or back in the box does not change it either. Moving back gives a bit more time. Moving up lets you meet the pitch before it moves as much. Either way, the front foot is still your reference.

Q11. How do I stay connected with my lead arm?

A. The lead arm is the arm closest to the pitcher. Its hand is usually the bottom hand on the bat: the left hand for a right-handed hitter. Think of it as the steering wheel. Your body is the engine. Great bat speed does not help if the steering pulls the barrel off its path.

The lead arm's job is to keep the hands connected to the torso, guide the barrel into the pitch's plane, control the swing's direction, keep the hands from leaving the body too early, and keep you "inside" the ball. Inside the ball means the barrel comes from behind the ball. It does not mean pinning the hands to the body. The arm stays connected but not stiff or locked.

The common mistake is pushing the hands. The front foot lands, the hands disconnect, the lead arm pushes forward, the barrel stays behind the hands, and you try to force it to the ball late. That narrow path cuts across the pitch. It causes weak grounders, pull-side rollovers, hooked fouls, lower bat speed, and trouble with off-speed.

The better sequence:
1. Load into the rear hip.
2. Keep the hands near the rear shoulder.
3. Stride and land balanced.
4. Rotate with the lower body and torso.
5. Let the rear shoulder and rear elbow turn the barrel into the slot.
6. Keep the lead hand connected to the body's rotation.
7. Guide the barrel through the ball.
8. Release the barrel naturally after the body turns toward the pitcher.

The body's rotation carries the hands. The lead hand steers.

Willy Adames example: before the fix, his hands separated from his body and left a visible gap. His swing worked around his body, an east-to-west path, instead of entering from behind the ball. After he connected the lead arm, hands, and torso, he stayed inside the ball, stopped pushing his hands, got the barrel into the zone earlier, stayed through contact longer, hit more to the middle, and rolled over less. The fix came from letting the lead arm move with the body, not from making it stiff.

Drill: the Roll Drill (Episode 9) trains this connection.

Q12. How do I get on plane with the ball?

A. Getting on plane means turning the barrel so its path starts matching the incoming pitch's path before contact, then keeping it on that path as long as possible. A pitch travels slightly downward into the plate. The barrel should enter that path and ride with it, not cross it for one instant. This gives you a longer contact window, more time to adjust to off-speed, and a better chance to drive the ball.

The G-Swing sequence in one line: "Scap load, roll into the slot, create lag, and accelerate the barrel through the pitch."

Step by step:
1. Begin balanced and connected. Athletic stance, hands near the rear shoulder.
2. Load the rear scapula. As you stride, pull the rear shoulder blade slightly toward the spine. At landing the hands are near the rear shoulder, the rear elbow is behind the body, the bat is close to your center, and you are balanced.
3. Roll the top forearm. It turns the barrel behind you into the pitch's plane, palm up like serving a pizza. This is not rolling the wrists over the ball.
4. Roll the barrel into the slot. The slot is the connected spot where the rear elbow works near your side, the hands stay connected, and the barrel turns behind the hands.
5. Create barrel lag. The barrel briefly trails the hands and the turning body, ready to accelerate. Do not make lag by dragging the hands forward.
6. Accelerate the barrel forward through the zone and through the field, not down at the ball. Finish strong.

Avoid three common cues taken the wrong way: "be short to the ball" (taking the hands straight at the ball makes a tiny window), "get on top of the ball" (palm down, chopping, negative attack angle), and "take the knob to the ball" (hands move first, barrel gets left behind). Check your video for the wrong path: the hands move first, the top palm turns down, the barrel gets steep, cuts across the pitch, and leaves the zone fast.

Why it helps against off-speed: on plane, the barrel stays available longer, so you can delay the final acceleration and still square a changeup or a breaking ball moving down and away.

Q13. What is the good directivity of bat path?

A. Good directivity means the barrel, and so the ball, travels toward the part of the field you intend instead of cutting across toward the pull side.

What it looks like on the bat path: the barrel gets onto the pitch's plane before contact, stays through the hitting zone, and then accelerates forward through the field, not down at the ball. The wrong path is the opposite: the hands move first, the top palm turns down, the barrel gets steep, cuts across the pitch, and leaves the zone quickly.

The checkpoint at contact is the money gap: top palm up, bottom palm down, arms connected to the body, and space between the forearms, with the barrel moving through the ball's intended direction. That palm-up, palm-down position delays the rollover. An early rollover (the top hand turning over too soon) makes the barrel cut across the ball and produces weak pull-side grounders or hooked fouls. Do not hold the position after contact. The forearms turn over naturally once the barrel is through the ball.

How to train it: the top-hand and bottom-hand drills. The body creates the direction, the shoulder stays connected, and the hand finishes the action. To send the ball to center, land balanced, move the back shoulder through center field, and release toward the middle.

Avoid: rolling the top hand before contact, pulling with only the arms, pushing the bottom hand away from the body, letting the hands go before the lower body turns, and spinning so hard that the barrel cuts to the pull side right away.

Q14. Why do I keep popping the ball up?

A. Pop-ups happen when the barrel meets the ball too far below its center. The launch angle is very high but the exit velocity is low. Coaches usually say you are "under the ball" or "off the plane."

Common causes:
1. Swinging down. A steep downswing crosses the ball's path for only a moment. A small timing miss and you clip under it. Hitting down also creates too much backspin, so the ball goes straight up.
2. Chasing lift. Dropping the back shoulder, lowering the hands, leaning back, or scooping puts the barrel under the ball. A very high launch angle can simply be a weak pop-up.
3. Hands going first. When the hands move before the barrel turns into place, the barrel gets steep and leaves the zone quickly, so timing errors turn into mishits.

Where you hit the ball matters:
- Too high on the ball: ground ball.
- Center: hard line drive.
- Slightly below center: line drive with lift.
- Too far below center: pop-up.

The fix:
- Use a small, positive attack angle. The barrel moves forward and slightly up, a shallow rise, not an uppercut.
- Get on plane early. Scap load, roll the barrel into the slot, create lag, then accelerate through the pitch.
- Aim for solid contact near the center of the ball. Line drives of about 10-20 degrees are a better goal than lifting everything.
- Do not chase useful backspin. It comes from a barrel moving through the ball with contact just below center.

Q15. I keep rolling over and hitting weak ground balls to the pull side. What is wrong?

A. You are rolling over. Your top hand turns over before contact, so the barrel cuts across the ball instead of moving through it. The ball gets hit on its top half and goes into the ground toward the pull side.

Why it happens:
1. Top hand hammers down. Trying to "get on top of the ball" makes the top forearm pronate (palm turns down) too early. The barrel gets steep and crosses the pitch's path. Results: topped grounders, pull-side rollovers, hooked fouls, and a short hitting zone.
2. Hands push and the lead arm disconnects. You land and shove both hands toward the pitcher. The barrel stays behind the hands, then gets whipped across the ball late.
3. Contact too far out front. Reaching way out in front, especially on inside pitches, means the barrel is already turning sharply toward the pull side when it meets the ball.

How each part fixes it:
- Swing path: get on plane. Scap load, roll the barrel into the slot, create lag, then accelerate through the ball and through the field, not down at it.
- Lead arm: think steering wheel. Keep it connected to the turning torso so the body carries the hands. The lead hand guides the barrel through the ball's direction instead of pulling it around.
- Top hand: "Connect, roll, deliver the pizza." At contact the top palm is up, the bottom palm is down, with space between the forearms. Let the hands turn over only after the barrel is through the ball.

What it does to contact point and launch angle: a rollover makes contact out front and on top of the ball. That gives a low or negative launch angle (below 0 degrees means the ball is driven into the ground) and weak exit velocity. Fixing the path moves contact back to the front-foot window and lets you meet the ball near center. That turns rollovers into line drives in the 10-20 degree range, aimed up the middle.

Drills: the Roll Drill (Episode 9) for connection and the Direction Drill (Episode 7) for top-hand control.

Q16. What is the batting kinetic chain sequence?

A. The kinetic chain runs from the ground up. Each body part turns in order and the bat comes last.

1. Trigger and timing. A first move sets your rhythm and catches the pitch's timing. Examples are a bat wiggle, a foot tap, or a big leg kick. Once it starts, the movement flows from the bottom up: knee rotation, then hip rotation, then rotation of the chest (thorax) and shoulders.
2. Launch (power) position. That sequence brings you to the launch or power position, ready to strike.
3. Execution. When you decide to swing, the front leg lands. Then the foot turns, then the knee, then the hip, and finally the upper body. Only after this chain does the bat swing out.
4. The "back space." About 80% of the mechanism happens before the bat reaches the ball. The keys are the hip hinge (to use the pelvis fully) and upper-lower body separation (twisting the body for maximum torque). Juan Soto is an example: he holds his power on the back-leg axis while keeping a big upper-body twist.
5. Delivery style. Energy moves either by an active forward weight shift (like Yordan Alvarez) or by rotating on the back-leg axis (like Bryce Harper). The back-leg style is common in big home-run hitters like Aaron Judge and Shohei Ohtani.
6. Path and impact. Impact and follow-through are extensions of the flow, not the source of power. Many players describe a "swing down" feeling, but the barrel actually travels a circular, slightly upward path. The feeling comes from the elbow driving down toward the body. Shoulder depth shapes the trajectory. A shallow shoulder entry (Ronald Acuña Jr.) leads to lower launch angles and line drives. A deeper entry (Aaron Judge) lets the hitter get under the ball for higher launch angles and more home runs.

Q17. Should I swing down on the ball?

A. No. Do not swing down on the ball.

The pitch is already traveling downward when it reaches you. A downward bat path crosses the pitch's path instead of riding with it, like two roads meeting at an intersection. The bat and ball share the same space for only an instant. That is a negative attack angle. It produces topped and chopped ground balls, pull-side rollovers, weak contact, and pop-ups when the barrel cuts under the ball, because hitting down puts too much backspin on it. It also leaves almost no margin for timing errors. It may work against young defenders. It is not a reliable path to success at higher levels.

The cue "get on top of the ball" is often misread as "turn the top palm down and chop." That is the same mistake.

What to do instead: a small, positive attack angle. The barrel moves forward and slightly upward into contact, matching the pitch's plane and staying in the zone longer. This is a shallow rise, not a big uppercut. Do not scoop or lean back either.

One nuance: some big leaguers say they feel like they swing down. Their barrel actually travels a circular, slightly upward path. The "down" feeling comes from the elbow driving down toward the body, not from the barrel chopping at the ball.

Q18. How do I get to the ball strong enough with my bat or hands?

A. Get to the ball with your body, not by throwing your hands at it. Pushing the hands toward the ball feels quick but makes a weak, narrow path.

1. Separate the right way. As you stride, load the rear shoulder blade (scap load) and keep the shaft of the bat close to your rear shoulder, knob toward the catcher. Do not walk the hands away from your body. A bat that stays near your center of mass gives more consistent, stronger swings against both fastballs and off-speed.
2. Let the body carry the hands. Your lower body and torso are the engine. The lead arm is the steering wheel. Keep the lead arm connected to the turning torso and let the rear shoulder and rear elbow turn the barrel into the slot. Then guide the barrel through the ball. If you land and push both hands forward, the barrel stays behind the hands. You lose bat speed and hit weak grounders, rollovers, and hooked fouls.
3. Use a path that stays in the zone. A barrel that moves from low to forward and slightly upward matches the pitch's downward plane and keeps the bat in the hitting zone longer. That gives you more chances at solid contact. It is not a dramatic uppercut.

Q19. What should the hip load do?

A. The hip load puts your weight and tension into the rear hip before the swing. It should do two things.

1. Give you balance. Loading into the rear hip lets you stay balanced for a moment while you track the pitch. You control your timing instead of rushing forward.
2. Give you power. When the rear glute and hamstring are engaged, the lower body is ready to rotate hard and create rotational power through the swing.

To make it work, hinge at the hips (move the rear hip back, slight knee bend) instead of bending only at the knees. Keep your weight inside the rear foot. It should not sway the body over the back leg, and it should not turn into a tall, quad-dominant knee bend.

Q20. How do I get into atheletic position and prepare to hit the ball?

A. Your athletic position is the hip hinge. It sets your timing and your power before the pitch arrives.

Get into it:
1. Start in a comfortable stance.
2. Shift your balance into the rear leg.
3. Bend both knees slightly.
4. Push the hips back, like closing a car door with your rear hip.
5. Keep the back strong and neutral. The chest can tilt forward a little.
6. Feel the rear glute and hamstring engage.
7. Stay balanced inside the rear foot.

This position lets you hold still for a moment and track the pitch instead of rushing. It also loads the glute and hamstring so the lower body can rotate with force. Do not sway over the back foot. Do not stand tall with the knees pushed forward (quad-dominant).

From there, be ready to swing on plane. The barrel should move from low, forward, and slightly upward to match the pitch's downward path and stay in the zone longer. On a low pitch that path helps you get under the ball for lift and backspin. On a high pitch you can meet the ball a bit farther out front while the barrel is still on its upward plane. The goal is an efficient swing that handles pitches at different heights, not a big uppercut.

Q21. How do I hit inside pitches?

A. Let your hands and barrel work through the hitting zone. Do not jam your hands inward or try to yank the ball. The barrel should travel forward with a slight upward turn, like a smooth Ferris wheel.

Meet the ball near the plane of your front foot, the same window as every other pitch. Turn a little tighter and keep your hands close to your body. Your timing decides the direction (right-handed hitter):
- Slightly late: right-center.
- On time: center.
- Slightly early: left-center.
- Too far out front: hooked foul.

The goal is a line drive into the pull-side gap, not a ball hooked foul past third. Reaching far out front also makes you cheat for the inside fastball, and then a changeup away beats you.

Drill: the Closed-Net ("phone booth") Drill. The net makes a tight space around you, like a phone booth.
1. Put a hitting net about three feet behind your normal hitting spot.
2. Put home plate in the center of the box.
3. Take your normal stance.
4. Start with slow-motion swings.
5. Let the barrel turn into the zone and travel through the inside pitch.
6. Do not let the bat hit the net behind you.
7. Practice driving balls to the pull-side gap, center, and the opposite-field gap.

If your barrel gets long or swings too far behind you, it hits the net. The net also keeps your front shoulder from opening early. A compact, well-sequenced swing clears the net and goes through the ball. The goal is not to pull every inside pitch. It is to stay compact, meet the ball at the front-foot plane, and keep it fair.

Q22. How do I effectively load my hips before the swing?

A. Load by hinging at the hips, not by bending your knees.

Steps:
1. Start in a comfortable stance.
2. Shift your balance into the rear leg.
3. Bend both knees slightly.
4. Move the hips back as if closing a car door with your rear hip, or pulling the rear hip toward the dugout behind you.
5. Keep the back strong and neutral. Let the chest tilt forward a little from the hips, never by rounding the lower back.
6. Hold it for a moment and feel the rear glute and hamstring engage.
7. Keep the weight inside the rear foot.

It looks like the start of a Romanian deadlift (RDL). Keep it comfortable and athletic, not deep.

What makes it effective: you can stay balanced while you track the pitch and control your timing, and the loaded glute and hamstring give the lower body rotational power.

Check yourself for two mistakes:
- Sway: the head and upper body drift back, weight rolls to the outside of the rear foot, the front leg kicks up, and you have to rush forward. Make the move smaller. Load into the hip instead of leaning over the leg.
- Quad-dominant: hips stay tall, chest upright, rear knee drives over the toes, and you feel nothing in the glute or hamstring. Let the rear hip move back instead.

Q23. Pitchers keep pounding my weak spot low and away. Should I give up the low and away and focus on hitting other spots?

A. For most hitters, yes. Concede the cold zone at first and play to your strength. That is the orthodox answer.

Shohei Ohtani is the example. Almost every square of his zone is hot. His one cold zone is low and away, so pitchers work low and away and avoid up and in. That is where his strikeouts come from.

Why giving it up works: a pitcher cannot throw all three strikes to one spot. They have to throw setup pitches. The battle moves into the sequence. They might finish you low and away, use low and away to get ahead and then finish inside, or throw to your favorite spot but jam it for a foul. Your default stays the same: time the fastball, and do not miss the pitch in your own location.

There is a second, harder option for better hitters. Hunt the pitch in your cold zone on purpose, either by drawing the ball there or by predicting when it is coming. Clutch spots often demand this, because no pitcher gives you anything easy when runs are needed.

So start by owning your strengths. Work toward reading when the low-away pitch is coming so you can sit on it.

Q24. After two breaking balls miss badly and I am 0-2, should I sit fastball and forget the last pitch?

A. Sitting fastball after two breaking balls miss is the classic read. Forgetting the last pitch is the risky part. The pitcher and catcher build the next pitches on what you just saw.

The source case is Christian Walker against Nathan Eovaldi (2023 World Series). Walker is a strong fastball hitter who is weaker on breaking balls. Texas started with a curveball off the plate away, then a splitter that missed badly. Both were balls, so the count was 2-0 (two balls, no strikes), a hitter's count. Eovaldi had to throw a fastball, and Walker knew it.

What happened next:
- The fastball came, located perfectly down. Walker did nothing with it. 2-1.
- The catcher did not go back to a breaking ball. He changed height. Walker swung expecting a fastball up and got a cutter sliding just off it. 2-2.
- Then a 96 mph four-seamer on the outside corner. With the inside splitter still in his memory, Walker could not time it.

Lessons:
1. Yes, time the fastball in that spot. That is every hitter's default.
2. When the fastball you are sitting on arrives, you cannot miss it. That pitch was Walker's chance.
3. Do not assume the last pitch is gone. The battery changes height and location and uses your memory of earlier pitches to beat your timing.

Note: the source covers a 2-0 count. It does not cover being 0-2 (two strikes) after swinging through breaking balls.

Q25. Should I choke up a couple of fingers if I want more contact instead of max exit velo?

A. Yes, if contact is your goal. That is what Cody Bellinger did.

He moved his hands up the handle about two fingers' width. It is not an extreme choke-up. He tried it now and then in late 2022 and used it almost all the time in 2023. The effect: more barrel control and better contact.

His 2023 results, as he traded power for contact:
- Batting average: .210 (2022) to .305 (2023).
- Strikeout rate: about 27% down to about 17%.
- In-zone whiff rate: 22.4% down to 17.8%.
- Off-speed whiff rate: 31.4% down to 15.3%, hitting .412 against off-speed.
- Line-drive rate: 22.2% up to 26.6%.
- Average exit velocity: 89.4 mph down to 86.9 mph.

The lower exit velocity is the cost of the trade, not a problem. He stopped hunting home runs and started hunting contact. He also paired the choke-up with a flatter barrel angle at the trigger, which was the bigger change.

Q26. Will laying the barrel flatter at trigger help me catch changeups I keep missing?

A. It can. In Cody Bellinger's case it was the bigger of his two changes, and better handling of off-speed pitches was the biggest reason his average came back.

What changed: right before the barrel turns, his bat used to sit nearly vertical. In 2023 he laid it off closer to flat.

Why it helps:
1. A flatter barrel shows more hitting surface to the ball, so you hit more line drives.
2. It removes a step. The bat head always comes forward as the upper body coils, so a vertical bat has to be flattened during the swing before it can be delivered. From a flatter start, the trigger fires and the barrel turns out from where it already is. No resetting, no pushing the barrel into place. That saves one or two video frames, and that was the decisive reason his average moved so much.

The off-speed numbers: his off-speed whiff rate was cut roughly in half, from 31.4% to 15.3%, and he hit .412 against off-speed pitches. He also choked up about two fingers' width at the same time.

Q27. Does tucking my back knee in help contact but make me late on real heaters?

A. Yes to both. That is Jung-Hoo Lee's trade-off.

The inward back knee is the traditional Japanese form. It favors precision over force. From that position the hip rotates out and he turns in place. There is no weight shift. He hits with rotational energy alone.

The upside: the back leg and hip travel a short distance. That lets him keep the contact point deep and hit compactly with impact. It is the source of his elite contact and his adjustment to off-speed.

The downside: premium velocity. A deeper contact point makes the best fastballs hard to cover. About 150 km/h (about 93 mph) is manageable. That is only the MLB average, though. Most teams' top two starters and most main relievers sit above 155 km/h (about 96 mph). "Deeper" here means only one or two balls' width, which is a big difference in baseball.

The comparison: a back knee lined up straight with the ground produces more force. Think of jumping off one leg. Nobody jumps with the knee caving in. It is hard to find an MLB hitter whose back leg is not set in line.

Q28. If I open my stance for more power, how do I keep my front shoulder from flying open?

A. That is the exact risk Jung-Hoo Lee ran into. Opening up let his front shoulder come open, and that was most likely why he went back to his old form.

His case: he kept an open stance but made it narrower and opened the shoulder angle a little more. The reason was not vision. His old setup coiled so far that gathering force into one place took too long. The old form's big strength was that his front shoulder stayed completely closed all the way through. That was the single biggest reason it never flew open. Once he relaxed that, the shoulder sometimes came open, which had never been seen from him before.

So whatever stance you use, the front shoulder has to stay closed through landing and into the turn:
- Balance before force (Max Muncy). Take a short stride, gather energy behind you, then push onto your center. Keep the shoulders closed until the rotational force reaches the upper body, so stored energy is not leaked early. Keep the head still.
- Closed-Net Drill (Episode 1). A net about three feet behind you makes a tight space that helps keep the front shoulder from opening up.

If the open stance keeps leaking your front shoulder, the Lee example says it may cost you more than the power it adds.

Q29. How do I spray the ball like a sprinkler instead of yanking everything pull side?

A. Build your approach on opposite-field hitting, the way Luis Arraez does. His nickname is La Regadera, "the sprinkler," because he can hit it anywhere.

Most hitters pull most of their balls because that is the direction of rotation and where force is easiest to make. Arraez, a left-handed hitter, does the opposite. His balls went about 38.9% to center, 32.6% to left (opposite field), and 28.5% to right. He works left-center and center on purpose. That is why he handles fastballs, breaking balls, and off-speed pitches.

How his swing makes it possible:
- Restraint. From stance to power position his hands barely move. The front leg going forward makes the energy. He builds almost no upper-body coil. The force comes from the weight shift.
- A short swing. His back elbow travels a short distance. Think of a boxing jab, not a big straight punch.
- Let the ball do the work. He puts the bat on the ball with only enough strength not to get overpowered. Balls die after clearing the infield, and his bat control threads balls down the lines.

The payoff is contact: about a 7.8% strikeout rate, about 95% contact on pitches in the zone, and about 89.5% contact on pitches outside it.

To practice going the other way, use the Angle Toss from the Off-Speed Pitch Approach note. For a right-handed hitter, the feeder tosses from the third-base side, so you have to let the ball travel and drive it to the opposite field.

Q30. If I practice hitting inside pitches the opposite side of pull side then what is the benefits?

A. The benefit is that the pitcher runs out of places to throw.

The usual teaching on inside pitches is to shorten the arms and pull it with a body turn. Luis Arraez trains the opposite. He practices taking inside pitches the other way and never just rides the rotation.

What it gets you:
1. A hit instead of a rollover. When a pitcher challenges him inside, the result is a hit rather than a weak pull-side grounder.
2. No safe spot for the pitcher. Against a hitter who goes the other way, coming inside is the obvious counter. If you can hit that pitch the other way too, the pitcher has nowhere left to go.
3. A bigger hitting window for off-speed. Once you can handle the inside fastball deep and to the opposite field, you no longer cheat forward to protect against it. Then off-speed pitches become chances: caught deep goes opposite field, caught off the front foot goes up the middle, caught early goes pull side.

Be honest about the difficulty. Most left-handed hitters trying this on an inside pitch struggle to get the ball past the shortstop. Train it with the Angle Toss. For a right-handed hitter, the feeder tosses from the third-base side, which forces you to let the ball travel, stay inside it, and drive it the other way.

Q31. If I am not a big guy, should I let go of the top hand after contact to add whip without losing contact?

A. It can work. Masataka Yoshida does it. The key is that the release happens after contact and your front hand is in control.

Yoshida is short with short arms. At contact his arms extend toward center field, and he lets go of the top hand almost every time. The reason is to make up for size. Releasing the top hand creates a longer swing arc, more centrifugal force, and a harder-hit ball. That offsets what he gives up in flexibility and raw strength. Yang Joon-hyuk's "banzai" finish works on the same idea.

It does not cost him contact because bat control lives in the front hand. His front-hand control is the best part of his swing. If your front hand cannot steer the barrel through the ball on its own, letting go of the top hand will hurt your contact instead of helping it.

Q32. How do I keep a short takeaway and still drive the ball toward center?

A. Follow Masataka Yoshida's model: compact going back, full extension coming through.

1. Short takeback. Take the bat back only a little, then come forward without changing the path of your arms. That makes a clean in-and-out route.
2. Let the torso drive it. A torso-driven swing magnifies that clean path. Core strength is what lets a hitter with short arms swing very compactly and still hit hard.
3. A slight upward path. Use the mild upward barrel path of a modern barrel hitter, nothing extreme.
4. Extend toward center. At contact, let your arms extend in a follow-through that drives the ball back up the middle rather than pulling it. Yoshida also releases his top hand after contact for a longer arc. That only works with strong front-hand control.

Q33. What is the money gap, and how does palm-up / palm-down stop me from rolling over too soon?

A. The money gap is the space between your forearms at contact and early in the follow-through. It is a checkpoint that shows you are squaring the ball and sending it in a good direction.

At contact you want:
- Top hand palm up.
- Bottom hand palm down.
- Arms connected to the body.
- Space between the forearms.
- The barrel moving through the ball's intended direction.

How palm-up, palm-down stops an early rollover: a rollover happens when the top hand turns over too soon. The barrel then cuts across the ball, which makes weak pull-side grounders or sharply hooked fouls. Keeping the top palm up and the bottom palm down at contact delays that turnover until the barrel has gone through the ball. That leads to more hard-hit balls and a better launch angle.

Do not hold the position after contact. The forearms turn over naturally once the barrel is through. The money gap is a checkpoint, not a pose. Train it with the top-hand and bottom-hand drills from the same episode.

Q34. How do the top-hand and bottom-hand drills teach me to send the ball the other way instead of hooking it foul?

A. Both drills teach you that the body sets the ball's direction and the hand only finishes it. When the arms pull on their own or the top hand rolls early, the barrel cuts across toward the pull side and hooks the ball foul. Directions below are for a right-handed hitter. Lefties reverse them.

Top-Hand Drill. Think of a third baseman turning a double play: the body starts it, the shoulder stays connected, the hand finishes.
1. Hold a baseball or soft ball in your top hand. Take an athletic stance facing a net or partner. No bat at first.
2. Load the rear shoulder blade without pulling the elbow far behind you.
3. Keep the top hand near the rear shoulder.
4. Rotate with the hips and torso.
5. Let the arm move into a compact throwing position.
6. Throw toward your target and finish with a controlled release.
Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mH0x1aptzog) at 1:39. In the swing, this keeps the top palm up at contact and stops early pronation, the rollover behind weak pull-side grounders.

Bottom-Hand Drill:
1. Hold a ball in your bottom hand, close to your body, arm connected to the torso. Pick a safe target such as a net.
2. Load into the rear hip.
3. Move forward and land balanced.
4. Rotate with the lower body and torso.
5. Let the back shoulder work toward the target.
6. Keep the bottom hand connected. Do not reach with the arm.
7. Release in the same direction your body is turning.
Reference video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mH0x1aptzog) at 3:01.

Why the ball goes where you aim: your release follows your rotation. To send it to center, land balanced, turn the back shoulder through center, and release toward the middle. Aim right-center to go the other way.

Avoid: rolling the top hand before contact, pulling with only the arms, pushing the bottom hand away from the body, letting the hands go before the hips turn, and spinning so hard that the barrel cuts to the pull side.

Q35. I struck out looking early. Should I still die with my plan instead of guessing next AB?

A. Yes. Stick with your plan.

Have a specific plan for every at-bat and stay with it. If you are going to fail, fail while executing your plan, not while guessing randomly. That was the core logic for David Ortiz and Manny Ramirez.

Baseball is a game of failure. Failing seven times out of ten still makes you great. Ortiz called Manny a "genius" because he did not care if he struck out early. He knew he would get that pitcher later in the game, or the next day.

Ortiz also says the analytics are only about 85% of it. The other 15% is mentality, what he calls the "Mamba mentality": handling adversity and staying hungry. One looking strikeout should not make you drop your plan.

Q36. Can I look lost on a pitch early so I can crush that same pitch later in the game?

A. Yes. Manny Ramirez did exactly that.

He used early at-bats to set pitchers up. He might take a strikeout or look "lost" on a certain pitch to build the pitcher's confidence in that sequence. Later in the game, when the pitcher went back to it, he "burned" them by hitting that same pitch "into the highway."

It works only because he had a specific plan and stuck to it. He did not care about striking out early, because he knew he would get the pitcher later. If you try it, go in with a clear plan for which pitch you are setting up. Do not just give away at-bats.

Q37. Why am I early on every changeup even when I try to sit off-speed?

A. You probably do not have an off-speed problem. You have a fastball problem.

Many hitters are taught to attack the inside fastball by getting the bat head out in front. Over time that becomes their only contact point, and every fastball gets pulled. The fastball is the fastest pitch you will see. If your timing is already committed out front for it, there is nowhere left to go when something slower comes. The changeup arrives after your swing is spent, even when you are trying to sit on it.

The fix is to build the biggest hitting window. Take away the pitcher's best weapon, the inside fastball, by letting it travel and driving it to the opposite field. Once you can do that, you stop cheating forward. Then the changeup becomes a chance:
- Caught deep: drive it to the opposite field.
- Caught off the front foot: drive it up the middle.
- Caught early: drive it to the pull side.
Every timing has a good result.

Drills, in this order:
1. Angle Toss. For a right-handed hitter, the feeder tosses from the third-base side. You have to let the ball travel and drive it the other way.
2. BOSU Ball Drill. Get down to the pitch with your legs, the inside of the back knee driving down, instead of reaching with your hands. Keep the bat shaft connected to your back shoulder.
3. V Drill. Lead arm holds the bat. The top hand opens into a V, palm up, off the handle. The back shoulder has to keep rotating, which stops the rollover and creates backspin when you are out front.

Avoid pulling every fastball, reaching with your hands for off-speed, and letting the hands finish the swing when the back shoulder stops.

Q38. How do I hit heaters without cheating out front so hanging breakers are still hittable?

A. Let the fastball travel, especially the inside one, and learn to drive it to the opposite field. Then you never have to cheat out front.

If you pull every fastball, your one contact point sits out in front of the plate. There is nothing left for a slower pitch. Once you can handle the inside fastball deep and to the opposite field, the worst case on that pitch is a ball dropped into the opposite field. You stop protecting against it, and your swing has room left for off-speed.

The contact-point map you want:
- Late or letting it travel: deep, back of the plate, to the opposite field.
- On time: off the front foot, to the middle.
- Early: in front of the plate, to the pull side.
You do not pick the destination ahead of time. You build a swing that gives a good result at every point.

With hanging breakers, a hanging breaking ball in the middle of the zone does not need a special move. Just stay ready for it. If you are early on a breaker, keep the back shoulder turning with the top palm up (the V Drill). You get backspin, which turns an early swing into a line drive over the shortstop, a gap double, or a pull-side home run instead of a grounder to third.

Drill order: Angle Toss (direction first), then BOSU Ball Drill (lower half), then V Drill (upper half and finish).

Q39. If I time every at-bat to the fastball, where should my hands start?

A. Near your chest. Corey Seager times every plate appearance to the fastball. He sets his hands near the chest, where they can be drawn fastest and where a human naturally produces force.

The rest of his setup supports it. He uses a small front-foot tap as a trigger instead of a high leg lift. His stride is aggressive while his weight stays back. The barrel comes around wide instead of stabbing at the ball, so the bat face can match the roughly 5-degree angle most pitches have at the front of the plate.

Q40. How do I stride hard without leaking my weight onto the front side too soon?

A. Copy Corey Seager's pattern. Use a small trigger, stride far, and keep the weight back.

1. Trigger with a tap. Start the swing with a tap of the front foot, not a high leg lift.
2. Ordinary width, aggressive stride. His stance and stride width are normal. The stride itself is aggressive, and the front leg goes far forward.
3. Do not let the weight follow the foot. The front leg strides out but the weight never leaks with it. That is what holds the power position.
4. Unwind in order. From that power position the coiled body unwinds knee, then pelvis, then torso, then shoulders.

Q41. Can I still hit for power with a flatter path if I keep weight back and take a long stride?

A. Yes. Giancarlo Stanton does exactly that. But his swing runs on rare raw strength, and it costs a lot of strikeouts.

His pattern:
1. Closed stance with a front-foot timing tap and a toe step.
2. A very good hip hinge at setup. After the stride, his arms are still where they started.
3. A small coil. He has noticeably less upper-lower separation than other home-run hitters.
4. A long stride that makes up for it. The front foot travels a long way, which thickens the coil instead of sharpening it.
5. A flat path, not an uppercut. It is a slight, smooth upward path. His shoulders do not drop as low as Aaron Judge's or Mike Trout's.
6. Weight stays back, up to about 60% behind, even with that wide stride. The front leg brakes firmly and the back foot scissors slightly, which adds force.

The image is a heavy polearm, not a blade: thick and solid rather than quick. It works because of his size, about 2 meters and over 110 kg, and his strength. His average exit velocity is around 93 mph, in the top 1% every year since 2015. The cost is a high strikeout rate and a low contact rate.

Q42. If my exit velo is huge, why do I still strike out so much on a max-effort swing?

A. Because the swing that produces that exit velocity does not change with two strikes.

Giancarlo Stanton averages around 93 mph off the bat. He has been in the top 1% every year since tracking began in 2015. Exit velocity matters, because hit and home-run chances rise with it. But he takes the same max-effort swing in every count. A high strikeout rate and a low contact rate are the necessary result. This season he struck out 28% of the time, versus about 22% for Pete Alonso and 24% for Max Muncy.

It has worked for him because a career on-base percentage around .353 and steady home runs carry the strikeouts. When he barrels the ball, it is devastating.

Another factor is elasticity. Compared with 2017, his 2022 swing looks stiffer, whether from age or heavy lifting. That stiffness likely makes adjusting harder and his reactions later. More strikeouts follow.

Q43. If I lengthen my stride to hit breaking balls, will I get late on heaters?

A. That is what happened to Gunnar Henderson. The longer stride fixed breaking balls and opened a hole against fastballs.

He widened his stride and weight transfer by about one foot's width. Nothing else changed. The bigger weight shift gave him a sturdier base, a wider timing window, and a visibly stronger swing.

Against breaking balls, he went from a .237 average, .479 slugging, and .330 wOBA to .333, .870, and .537. Against off-speed, from .241, .483, and .307 to .286, .612, and .573.

The cost: timing set for spin arrives slightly late on fastballs, especially in unfavorable counts. Against fastballs his average fell from .250 to .194, his whiff rate rose from 27.2% to 29.1%, and his strikeout rate rose from 26% to 32.1%.

His overall 24% strikeout rate is not the issue. Aaron Judge is at 24.4%. Getting beaten by the pitch he sees most often is.

Q44. How do I keep a bigger weight shift without getting beaten by fastballs in 0-2?

A. The Henderson note describes this problem but does not give a fix.

The problem: Gunnar Henderson widened his stride and weight shift by about one foot's width. That improved his breaking-ball and off-speed numbers and his power. But his timing is now set for spin. He arrives slightly late on fastballs, especially in unfavorable counts like 0-2, which is exactly where his breaking-ball work improved most. His fastball average fell from .250 to .194 and his strikeout rate on fastballs rose from 26% to 32.1%.

The closest answer in the notes is Seiya Suzuki's adjustment against major-league velocity. He moved his contact point earlier, shortened his stride, and started his hands earlier to buy a step of preparation. The shorter stride costs some coil and weight transfer. That is the trade you would make in two-strike counts.

Q45. Can I still get on base if I hunt pull-side homers almost every swing?

A. Yes, if you walk a lot. That is how Kyle Schwarber does it.

He batted .201 but had a .349 on-base percentage, a gap of about 148 points. He had 106 hits (43 home runs) and 116 walks, more walks than hits.

His approach has three deliberate choices:
1. Pull. He sets his contact point about one ball's width in front of normal. About 60% of his balls are pulled and only about 17% go the other way.
2. Guess. He has huge strength but limited bat control, so he picks a location and pitch type before the at-bat and times everything to the fastball.
3. Swing aggressively. If it is his pitch, he swings from the first pitch in any count, especially at high and up-and-in pitches.

The walks come from two things. He owns a zone and ignores pitches outside it. Pitchers fear his power, so they nibble at the edges and miss.

The cost: he is weak against breaking balls (especially sliders that tunnel off the fastball) and off-speed. His whiff rate is in the bottom 17% of the league, and he strikes out a lot. It is mostly walk, strikeout, or home run. Without strict zone discipline and real power, this approach does not get you on base.

Q46. How do I own a personal zone so I walk a lot even when my average is low?

A. Decide on your zone before the pitch and refuse everything outside it, the way Kyle Schwarber does.

1. Own a zone. If a pitch is not in the zone he has set for himself, he has no interest in it. He is aggressive and patient at the same time. He will even take a strike in the zone without flinching if it is not the pitch he came to hit. Only with two strikes does he loosen that rule.
2. Make the pitcher pay for mistakes. Pitchers know that if they miss over the plate, the ball leaves the park. So they work the edges, miss outside, and walk him. His walks are partly earned and partly given.

The result: a .201 average but a .349 on-base percentage, with 116 walks against 106 hits. The second part depends on real power. Pitchers only nibble at hitters they fear.

Q47. If my batting average is not high, can pitch selection still make my power play?

A. Yes. That is Max Muncy's whole case.

Hitting for power needs two skills. One is contact. The other is pitch judgment: knowing which pitch you can punish and waiting for it, like a sniper. A hitter with real power needs only one of the two to survive. Hitters with both, like Aaron Judge, Mike Trout, and Yordan Alvarez, run the league.

Muncy survives on judgment. He is a career .231 hitter, around .250 even in his best years. But his eight-season on-base percentage is .357, a gap of about 125 points over his average. This season it is .254 with a .411 on-base percentage. For comparison, Judge's and Trout's gaps are about 110 points and Ohtani's about 91.

That patience sits on top of real power: 35 home runs in 2018, 35 in 2019, 12 in shortened 2020, 36 in 2021, and 21 in 2022 while playing hurt. Waiting for your pitch lets your power play even when your average is low.

Q48. How do I keep my head still and front shoulder closed while I transfer weight?

A. Use Max Muncy's rule: balance before force.

1. Take a short stride.
2. Gather your energy behind you.
3. Push forward onto your center axis.
4. Keep the shoulders closed until the rotational force reaches the upper body. If the front shoulder opens early, the energy you stored leaks out before you can use it.
5. Keep the head still the whole time.

The head is the non-negotiable part. If the head moves, mechanics are no longer the conversation. The swing has to be rebuilt from scratch.

---

Q49. I already hit the ball hard. Should I tilt more upper swing just to chase home runs?

A. It is a trade, not a free upgrade. Mike Trout made this exact move.

For most of his career Trout swung close to level. He turned his strength into hard contact and waited for his zone and his pitch. That made him a high-OPS, gap-to-gap hitter rather than a pure slugger.

In 2022 he tilted the swing upward into a home-run path. Ground balls and line drives dropped, fly balls rose, he swung more often, and he still hit the ball harder. It was his best home-run rate per plate appearance ever.

The cost was contact: more whiffs, more strikeouts, fewer walks, and real ups and downs from game to game for a hitter who used to have almost none. If you already hit the ball hard, a more upward path can add home runs. Decide whether you can give up that contact and those walks.

---

Q50. If I swing more for fly balls, what do I give up in contact and walks?

A. Based on Mike Trout's 2022 change, you give up contact, walks, and consistency.

When he tilted his path upward for more fly balls, he hit more home runs per plate appearance than ever. But he also had:
- More swings and misses.
- More strikeouts.
- Fewer walks.
- Fewer ground balls and line drives.
- More game-to-game swings in results, from a hitter who used to have almost none.

He also swung more often overall. That added home runs but cut into the patient approach that had made him a high-OPS hitter.

---

Q51. Fastballs keep beating me. Do I just move my contact point earlier?

A. Moving your contact point earlier is part of it. Seiya Suzuki did exactly that against major-league velocity, but he changed his whole timing, not just the contact spot.

His rookie year in Chicago: a .260s average, 14 home runs, a 24% strikeout rate, and an 89.6 mph average exit velocity, middle of the league. He found MLB velocity harder than expected.

What he changed:
1. Contact point. He named this himself. He pulled his timing a beat or two earlier.
2. Shorter stride. It costs some coil and some weight transfer but buys a step of preparation.
3. Earlier hands. His hands started close to a full tempo sooner on video.
Nothing else needed touching. He also got healthy and built a routine that fit him.

One caution: earlier timing is not the same as reaching far out front. Reaching way out front, especially on inside pitches, hooks balls foul and leaves you exposed to changeups away. Keep the front-foot contact window and get ready sooner.

---

Q52. Is a shorter stride worth it if it gets me ready one beat sooner?

A. For Seiya Suzuki, it was. After a rookie year where major-league velocity beat him (24% strikeout rate, 14 home runs), he shortened his stride along with moving his contact point earlier.

The trade is clear. A shorter stride costs some coil and some weight transfer. In return it buys a step of preparation. He paired it with starting his hands earlier, close to a full tempo sooner on video. Nothing else in his mechanics changed, because they were never the problem.

It is worth it if being late on fastballs is your real problem. You give up a little stored power to be ready on time.

---

Q53. How do I get power from the front knee without loading my hands extra?

A. Follow Shohei Ohtani's minimalist swing. Keep the hands quiet and let the legs make the power.

1. Simple start. From the stance, when the ball comes, the swing just goes.
2. Toe step. Plant the front leg forward. The hands stretch only as far as the front leg moves. Nothing pulls farther back and nothing drops.
3. Power from the front knee. The pelvis folds, both legs settle slightly toward the ground, and the hips push a little forward. That builds the power position.
4. Rotation. The front knee, which had come in, turns outward to create rotational force. The back knee turns the same way at the same time, and the pelvis turns with them. That sends force to the point of contact.
5. Arms. The elbow tucks in tight, and the angle between the hands on the bat and the forearm is kept.

---

Q54. Why keep the rear-elbow angle instead of fully extending through contact?

A. Holding that angle is what keeps the power in the swing. In Shohei Ohtani's swing, the elbow tucks in as tight as possible, but the angle between the hands on the bat and the forearm stays the same. American coaching treats holding that angle through the finish as essential for producing power. Hitters are not taught to straighten the arms.

The bat-path note explains why. The power comes from the body turning while the barrel lags behind and then accelerates through the pitch, without an early, isolated wrist flick. If you fully straighten out early, you release that angle before the barrel gets through the ball, and the force leaks out.

---

Q55. If I am fast, does raising my walk rate matter more than rebuilding my swing?

A. If your swing is already sound, getting on base more is what turns your speed into value. That was Elly De La Cruz's leap.

His speed is elite. His 30.5 ft/sec sprint speed led MLB, and he covered 90 feet in 3.67 seconds. As a rookie he hit .235 with a .710 OPS and an 84 wRC+, below average, even though his swing was already stable.

The real improvement in year two was plate discipline. His walk rate rose from 8.2% to 14.4%, about six points higher, and his on-base and slugging both went up. Through 29 games he was hitting .280 with a .980 OPS and 18 steals. A higher on-base percentage turns his best tool loose. At .280 with a .390-.410 on-base percentage, people started talking about Rickey Henderson's 130-steal record.

The note does not say to never work on your swing. It shows that for a fast player, more times on base pays off quickly.

---

Q56. Should I drop my weak side as a switch hitter to protect that speed edge?

A. The notes lean yes when the gap is as big as Elly De La Cruz's. They also warn it is hard to do.

His split as a rookie (2023): left-handed against righties, 70 hits in 305 at-bats, 11 home runs, a .799 OPS. Right-handed, 21 hits in 114 at-bats, 2 home runs, a .184 average, a .495 OPS. It held in 2024: .311 with a 1.105 OPS from the left, .229 with a .729 OPS from the right.

Dropping the right side would help his hitting and protect his speed. Batting left starts you a step closer to first base. Among the fastest home-to-first runners, only Bobby Witt Jr. batted right-handed. But switch hitters rarely find it easy to give up a side.

---

Q57. Why would I leave an open stance that already made me mash?

A. Leave it only if it is causing a specific problem. Aaron Judge did not switch because square is "better."

He hit 62 home runs and, as a rookie, 52 from an open stance. He struggled in the World Series while already square. Open versus square is not the real question.

His reasons: you return to square to swing anyway. But starting about 20 degrees open meant that even after his leg lift he sometimes did not feel square, as if he was not facing the pitcher. Outside pitches seemed far away. Starting closer to square, about 5 degrees open, removed that feeling and improved his coverage away.

The deeper reason was how pitchers attacked him. Nobody wants to pitch Judge inside, so he saw a steady diet of pitches away, breaking balls off the outer edge, and more sweepers. He had a weakness, and he moved to close it. It worked: he came out of his 2024 slump, hit 58 home runs, and won the AL home-run title and MVP.

So keep the open stance unless pitchers are beating you somewhere it leaves exposed.

---

Q58. Everyone throws me away and sweepers. Should I start more square so outside pitches feel closer?

A. That is exactly why Aaron Judge went square.

Pitchers would not work him inside, so he got a steady diet of pitches away and breaking balls off the outer edge. The league's rise in sweepers made it worse. From his old stance, about 20 degrees open, he sometimes did not feel square even after his leg lift, and outside pitches felt far away. Starting closer to square, about 5 degrees open, removed that feeling and improved his coverage away.

It paid off: he came out of his 2024 slump with 58 home runs, the AL home-run title, and MVP.

Make the change for that reason, not because square is better in general. Judge also hit 62 home runs from the open stance.

---

Q59. Should I cut my swing rate and hunt one pitch per at-bat instead of attacking everything?

A. For Pete Crow-Armstrong, it turned his career around.

Pitchers attacked his free swinging with spin. Over about 70 games he hit .195 with a .533 OPS and a 47 wRC+, last among 149 qualified hitters. Alex Bregman's advice was simple: swing less. Stop attacking everything and hunt one pitch per at-bat.

The changes:
- Swing rate: 60% down to 49%.
- First-pitch swings: 53% down to 39%.
- Chase rate: 41.7% down to 34.1%.
- Walk rate: 4.5% up to 12%.

His home runs now come on timing he predicted instead of ambushes out in front. Along with a stance change and added strength, he hit .287 with a .388 on-base percentage, .545 slugging, 36 home runs, 30 steals, and a 156 wRC+ through 118 games.

---

Q60. Will standing a few inches closer to the plate help me against lefty stuff away?

A. It did for Pete Crow-Armstrong. Anthony Rizzo told him to stand about 7 cm (about 3 inches) closer to the plate.

The closer spot made outside pitches from left-handers easier to read. It also sped up his reaction to inside pitches. Against left-handed pitching he went from a .188 average and .593 OPS to .315 and .940.

He made this change along with hunting one pitch per at-bat and adding strength, so it was not the only reason his numbers improved.

---

Q61. Can I hit for power without sitting on my back leg like a classic slugger?

A. Yes. Ronald Acuña Jr. is the proof.

He does not sit on the back leg the way most home-run hitters do. He starts near a 50/50 balance and moves his weight forward. His elbow never opens the wide rear space power hitters use to build force. Still, in 2023 he hit 41 home runs with 73 steals, a .337 average, a 1.012 OPS, and a 170 wRC+. He is the only 40-70 player ever.

The kinetic-chain notes describe two ways to deliver energy. One is an active forward weight shift (like Yordan Alvarez). The other is rotating on the back-leg axis (like Bryce Harper, Aaron Judge, Shohei Ohtani). Both produce power.

The catch: Acuña's version works because it is paired with his eye, his restraint, and his elasticity. His shallow shoulder entry also tends to produce lower launch angles and line drives rather than high fly balls.

---

Q62. Why would a compact down-swing still work if my eyes and timing are good?

A. Because the swing's job is square, well-timed contact, and a compact path can deliver that when the eye and timing are elite.

Ronald Acuña Jr.'s swing stabs in compactly, in the down-swing lineage rather than the uppercut now standard in MLB. On paper that looks dated. Combined with his eye, his restraint, and his elasticity, it works: a .337 average, 41 home runs, and a 170 wRC+.

Two things keep it productive:
1. The money gap. Top palm up, bottom palm down at contact delays the rollover. That keeps the barrel from cutting across the ball and supports a high hard-hit rate and a good launch angle.
2. The "down swing" is often a feeling. Many big leaguers describe swinging down, but the barrel still travels a circular, slightly upward path. The feeling comes from the elbow driving down toward the body. A shallow shoulder entry like Acuña's leads to lower launch angles and line drives.

A caution: a truly downward barrel path (negative attack angle) crosses the pitch for only an instant. It produces topped grounders and rollovers and leaves little room for timing errors. Keep the compact feel, but the barrel still needs to get on plane.

---

Q63. If my attack angle is already positive, why do I still hit ground balls and pop-ups?

A. A positive attack angle is only part of the answer. You also have to square the ball.

Attack angle is the direction of the barrel before and during contact. Launch angle is the direction of the ball after contact. Where the barrel meets the ball decides the launch angle:
- Contact above the ball's center: ground ball.
- Centered contact: hard line drive on a lower trajectory.
- Slightly below center: line drive with lift.
- Too far below center: pop-up.

So a hitter with a good, slightly upward path can still top the ball or get under it. The fix is squaring the ball, not swinging more uphill. The note warns against chasing launch angle by dropping the back shoulder, lowering the hands, leaning back, scooping, or making an exaggerated uppercut. A very high launch angle can simply be a weak pop-up.

---

Q64. What is the 'two roads crossing at an intersection' comparison for chopping down on the ball?

A. It explains why chopping down gives you so little chance at solid contact.

The pitch travels down one road. A steep, downward bat path cuts quickly across it, like a second road crossing at an intersection. The bat can still hit the ball, but the two paths share the same space for only a moment.

A positive attack angle works more like two roads running side by side. The barrel travels with the descending pitch, stays in the hitting zone longer, and leaves more room for timing mistakes.

Chopping down (a negative attack angle) tends to produce:
- Topped ground balls.
- Weak contact.
- Rollovers.
- Backspin pop-ups when you cut under the ball.
- A small margin for timing errors.

---

Q65. What is the 'back space' in hitting, and why is 80% of the swing said to happen there?

A. The back space is everything that happens before the bat reaches the ball. Modern batting theory says about 80% of the hitting mechanism happens there. Contact and follow-through are treated as extensions of that flow, not the source of power.

The key pieces in the back space:
- Hip hinge, to make full use of the pelvis.
- Upper-lower body separation: twisting the body to build torque (rotational force).
- Weight transfer.

Examples from the note:
- Juan Soto holds power firmly on his back-leg axis while keeping a big upper-body twist.
- Energy gets delivered one of two ways: an active forward weight shift (Yordan Alvarez) or rotating on the back-leg axis (Bryce Harper).
- The back-leg style also shows up in big home run hitters such as Aaron Judge and Shohei Ohtani.

---

Q66. Why do hitters like Alex Rodriguez say they swing down when the bat actually travels up?

A. It's a feeling, not the real bat path.

Players such as Alex Rodriguez and Jose Ramirez describe "swinging down." In reality the barrel follows a circular, slightly upward path. The down feeling comes from the elbow driving down toward the body as the barrel turns.

So the cue can describe what the rear elbow feels like. Taken literally, though, chopping the barrel down at the ball creates a negative attack angle. That leads to topped ground balls, rollovers, and a short contact window.

---

Q67. How does shoulder depth change launch angle, and how do Ronald Acuña Jr. and Aaron Judge compare?

A. Shoulder depth is how low the shoulders enter as the swing turns. It helps decide the bat's trajectory.

- A shallower shoulder entry, like Ronald Acuña Jr., leads to lower launch angles and line drives.
- A deeper shoulder entry, like Aaron Judge, lets the hitter "dig under" the ball. That gives higher launch angles and more home runs.

Neither is called wrong. They produce different batted-ball profiles. Launch angle is the vertical direction of the ball right after contact, measured in degrees.

---

Q68. What is a south-to-east swing, and how does it help on both low and high pitches?

A. In Swing 101, a south-to-east swing is a barrel that moves from low to forward and slightly upward. It matches the pitch's descending plane and keeps the bat in the hitting zone longer.

- On a low pitch, it lets you get underneath the ball and create lift and backspin.
- On a high pitch, you can meet the ball farther out front while the barrel is still traveling on its upward plane.

The goal is not a dramatic uppercut. It is an efficient, on-plane swing that handles pitches at different heights. Other notes describe the same idea as a south-to-north or Ferris-wheel path.

---

Q69. If chopping down works in youth leagues, why doesn't it hold up at higher levels?

A. Chopping down can produce ground balls that sneak through young defenders. Against better fielders it isn't a reliable path to success.

The pitch is already traveling downward. A downswing crosses the ball's path only briefly, so solid contact is less likely. It tends to produce ground balls, rollovers, or pop-ups.

Swing 101 also points out that hitting the ball in the air creates more chances for extra-base hits and home runs. Its examples show hitters producing more as they raised their average launch angle.

The better path is low to forward and slightly upward, matching the pitch's plane and staying in the zone longer.

---

Q70. What is the '15%' that David Ortiz says analytics can't give a hitter?

A. Ortiz splits modern hitting into roughly 85% analytics and 15% mindset. He argues the 15% is what actually drives success.

He calls it the "Mamba mentality" or "cojones": the internal drive to deal with adversity and the hunger to get things done. Technology can give you the 85%. It can't give you that drive.

It fits the rest of his view. Baseball is a game of failure, and failing seven out of ten times still makes you great. Have a plan for every at-bat, and if you fail, fail executing it instead of guessing.

---

Q71. Which three popular coaching cues can ruin my bat path?

A. The Bat Path note lists three cues that often get misread.

1. "Be short to the ball." Being efficient is good. Taking the hands and barrel straight at the ball is not. The barrel comes in steeply and passes through the pitch's path for an instant. Better: be short into the pitch's plane, then long through the hitting zone.

2. "Get on top of the ball." Hitters turn the top palm down and chop. That's a negative attack angle. You get topped grounders, pull-side rollovers, a barrel that's in and out of the zone fast, trouble with pitches down or away, and less ability to drive the ball.

3. "Take the knob to the ball." Driving the knob straight at the ball moves the hands forward before the barrel turns. The barrel trails the hands and has to catch up late. It feels quick, but the barrel isn't in the zone for long. The knob can stay on its track, just not as an isolated hand push.

---

Q72. How can I tell from video that my bat path is wrong?

A. Film your swing and look for this sequence. If you see it, your bat path is off:
1. The hands move first.
2. The top palm starts turning down.
3. The barrel gets steep.
4. The bat cuts across the pitch's path.
5. The barrel leaves the hitting zone quickly.

What good looks like at front-foot landing:
- Hands near the rear shoulder.
- Rear elbow organized behind the body.
- Bat close to your center.
- Front shoulder controlled.
- You're balanced and still able to adjust.

From there, the top forearm rolls the barrel into the slot, the barrel lags briefly, and then it accelerates forward through the field.

---

Q73. What is barrel lag, and how is it different from dragging the bat?

A. Lag means the barrel briefly trails the hands and the body's rotation while staying ready to accelerate. It comes naturally from the body turning while the forearms organize the barrel.

Dragging is different. Dragging is pulling the hands forward and leaving the barrel behind, or deliberately delaying the swing. Both the Bat Path and High Pitch notes warn against it.

A good lag position:
- The body has started rotating.
- The hands are still connected.
- The barrel is turned into a powerful position.
- The barrel hasn't been released early.
- It's ready to accelerate onto the pitch's plane.

After lag, the barrel accelerates forward through the zone without an isolated wrist flick.

---

Q74. Does standing at the front or back of the batter's box change my contact point?

A. No. Your personal contact reference stays the same: make contact near your front foot.

Moving in the box changes where you and your front foot sit relative to home plate. It doesn't change where the ball meets your body at contact.

- Moving back gives you slightly more time before the pitch reaches your front-foot plane.
- Moving forward lets you meet the pitch before it moves as much.

Either way, the front foot is still your main contact reference.

---

Q75. What does 'one contact window, three barrel directions' mean?

A. You meet every pitch at about the same depth, near the plane of your front foot, and change only where the barrel points. For a right-handed hitter:
- Inside pitch: turn tighter, keep the hands close, drive it to left-center.
- Middle pitch: through center field.
- Outside pitch: let the barrel work toward right-center.

The note's example is a 97 mph pitcher. First pitch, an inside fastball. A hitter who thinks inside pitches must be hit far out front starts early and hooks it foul. The pitcher sees him cheating. Second pitch, a changeup away. He's early again, so he reaches, rolls over, or misses.

The better hitter uses the front foot as the reference. He drives the inside fastball to left-center. He stays connected long enough to see the changeup and takes it to right-center in the same window. Location changes the barrel's direction, not the whole timing plan.

---

Q76. On an inside pitch, where does the ball go if I'm a little late, on time, or a little early?

A. For a right-handed hitter making contact near the front-foot plane:
- Slightly late: toward right-center.
- On time: toward center field.
- Slightly early: toward left-center.
- Too far out front: likely hooked foul.

Left-handed hitters reverse the directions.

That's why you shouldn't force the hands inward or try to pull the inside pitch. Let the hands and barrel work through the zone with a smooth, slightly upward Ferris-wheel turn, and your timing decides the field.

---

Q77. How far behind me does the net go in the Closed-Net drill, and what does it mean if my bat hits it?

A. Setup:
1. Place a hitting net about three feet behind your normal hitting position.
2. Put home plate in the center of the batter's box.
3. Take your normal stance.
4. Start with slow-motion swings.
5. Let the barrel turn into the zone and travel through the inside pitch.
6. Don't let the bat hit the net behind you.
7. Practice driving to the pull-side gap, center field, and the opposite-field gap.

If the bat hits the net, your barrel got long or traveled too far behind you. A compact, well-sequenced swing clears the net and works through the ball. The net also boxes you in, which helps keep your front shoulder from opening early.

The goal isn't pulling every inside pitch. It's staying compact, meeting the ball near the front-foot plane, and keeping it fair.

---

Q78. What does Mike Trout work on in his first round of batting practice?

A. In his first round, Trout often drives the ball toward right-center and right field. Then he works through the middle of the field, sometimes driving a ball to left-center to check that he's square to the pitcher and balanced.

What makes him exceptional is staying through the middle against pitches in every location. Inside, middle, or outside, he can drive it to left-center, center, or right-center with authority.

The lesson for an amateur isn't to copy his swing. Copy the approach: stay balanced, stay square to the pitcher, and build a barrel path that uses the whole field.

Q79. Who created the Drop-Bat drill, and do I actually drop the bat?

A. The source credits the Drop-Bat drill to hitting instructor Craig Wallenbach. You do not release or drop the bat. "Drop the bat" means letting the barrel settle into its working position before turning it through the zone.

Steps:
1. Take your normal stance.
2. A partner gives an easy front toss.
3. As the ball is tossed, let the barrel settle while keeping control of the bat.
4. Turn the barrel into the pitch's path.
5. Work from behind the ball, forward through the zone, and slightly up through contact.
6. Start slow. Add speed only when you can repeat it safely.

Reference video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Z5r9275-yM at 3:10 (Gradum Gswing).

It helps you let the ball travel, avoid rushing the hands, cut down pulled grounders and hooked fouls, and drive the ball to all fields.

Q80. What is the front-toss progression after the BOSU ball drill?

A. Once the BOSU and tee work feels right, take the training aids away and repeat the same movement against front toss:
1. Start with low-and-away tosses.
2. Let yourself recognize the moving ball.
3. Keep the same back-knee, back-hip, and rear-shoulder movement.
4. Keep the barrel working through the outer part of the plate.
5. Progress to middle-away, then high-away pitches.
6. Adjust the barrel angle to match each pitch height.

The goal is to carry the movement from a still ball to a moving one. Using the same barrel angle for high and low pitches is one of the listed mistakes.

---

Q81. What is the three-ball drill, and why do some coaches call it outdated?

A. The three-ball drill puts one ball out in front of the inside corner and one in the middle on the front part of the plate, and teaches hitting the outside pitch deep in the zone.

The Gradum G-Swing notes call this outdated. Pro hitters meet every pitch in about the same contact area: near the plane of the front foot after it lands. Only the barrel's direction changes.

Problems with the old way:
- Inside pitch too far out front: the barrel is already turning toward the pull side, so the ball hooks foul. You also start cheating for the inside fastball and can't adjust to a changeup or breaking ball away.
- Outside pitch too deep: at 95-98 mph you end up late. You get swings and misses, fouls straight back, weak opposite-field flies, contact near the handle, or no contact.

Q82. How do I set up the PVC pipe drill for hitting high pitches?

A. The PVC Pipe drill teaches you to match your shoulder plane to pitches at different heights.

Setup:
1. Use a lightweight PVC pipe about two inches in diameter.
2. Place it across or close to your shoulders.
3. Take a balanced batting stance.
4. A partner gives short, controlled front toss.
5. Use tennis balls only, never regulation baseballs, and clear the area.

Execution:
1. The partner tosses to a chosen part of the strike zone.
2. Use your legs and hip hinge to stay balanced.
3. Turn the torso and direct the back shoulder at the ball.
4. The pipe should travel through the ball's plane.
5. Keep rotating through the ball instead of stopping at contact.

Reference video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AyXaOtquLck at 1:52 (Gradum Gswing). Afterward, move to the T drill with a real bat and tee.

---

Q83. How high is too high to swing at a fastball?

A. As a training guideline, avoid chasing pitches above about belly-button height. The exact upper limit depends on your height, stance, and strike zone.

High fastballs are especially hard at high velocity. A pitch that looks hittable can keep carrying above your effective hitting zone.

Being able to hit a high pitch doesn't mean swinging at every elevated fastball. Learn to recognize a pitch above your preferred zone and let the umpire make the call.

---

Q84. What is the T drill for the high pitch?

A. After the PVC pipe drill, repeat the movement with a regular bat and a batting tee:
1. Start with the ball near the middle of the strike zone.
2. Move the tee gradually higher.
3. Keep your normal load and balanced landing.
4. Keep the top hand strong and connected.
5. Preserve barrel lag as the body starts rotating.
6. Match the back-shoulder angle and barrel path to the height of the ball.
7. Drive the ball on a firm line instead of trying to lift it.

High pitches need less shoulder tilt and a flatter barrel path. Don't reach up with the hands or stand up out of your stance. "Barrel lag" means the body starts turning while the barrel stays connected and ready, not dragging the bat.

---

Q85. What is a sway in the load, and how do I know if I'm doing it?

A. A sway is moving your whole body over or past the rear foot instead of loading into the rear hip.

Signs you're swaying:
- Your head and upper body drift backward.
- Your weight moves to the outside of the rear foot.
- Your front leg may kick up.
- You have to rush forward to recover.
- Timing and balance get hard to control.

The fix is a smaller movement. Pull the rear hip back into the hip socket and keep your body balanced inside the rear foot. Think "load into the rear hip," not "lean over the rear leg."

---

Q86. What does quad-dominant mean in a batting stance?

A. Quad-dominant means standing tall and pushing the knees forward, so the quadriceps (front of the thigh) take the load instead of the glutes and hamstrings. Bending only at the knees doesn't create an effective hip load.

Signs:
- Hips stay tall.
- Chest stays completely upright.
- Rear knee drives sharply forward.
- No tension in the rear glute or hamstring.
- The lower body pushes upward instead of rotating.

The fix is hinging at the hips. Move the hips back while keeping a slight knee bend, and keep the rear knee from driving way past the toes. Don't lock the knee rigid. The goal is a comfortable hinge, not removing all knee movement.

---

Q87. Why is the hip load compared to a Romanian deadlift or closing a car door?

A. Both pictures teach the same hip hinge.

In a Romanian deadlift (RDL), the hips move back, the knees stay slightly bent, and the torso tilts forward as one strong unit. "Closing a car door with your rear hip" is that same backward hip move.

Steps to find the athletic position:
1. Start in a comfortable stance.
2. Shift your balance into the rear leg.
3. Bend both knees slightly.
4. Move the hips back, as if closing a car door with your rear hip.
5. Keep your back strong and neutral, not rounded.
6. Hold briefly and feel the rear glute and hamstring engage.
7. Stay balanced inside the rear foot.

Don't go too deep. Joey Votto and Mike Trout look tilted forward because they hinge this way.

---

Q88. Why is the top-hand drill compared to a third baseman turning a double play?

A. On a double-play throw, the body starts the movement, the shoulder stays connected, and the hand finishes the action. The top hand works the same way in the swing.

Execution:
1. Hold a baseball or soft ball in your top hand. Athletic stance, facing a net or partner. No bat.
2. Picture yourself as a third baseman starting a double play.
3. Load the rear shoulder blade without pulling the elbow far behind you.
4. Keep the top hand near the rear shoulder.
5. Rotate with the hips and torso.
6. Let the arm move into a compact throwing position.
7. Throw at the target and finish with a controlled release.

Reference video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mH0x1aptzog at 1:39 (Gradum Gswing).

The point is a controlled barrel turn with the top palm up at contact. Turning it over before contact is an early rollover.

---

Q89. Should I hold my palm-up, palm-down hand position all the way through my follow-through?

A. No. Palm-up on the top hand and palm-down on the bottom hand is a checkpoint at contact, not a position to hold.

The forearms rotate naturally after contact. The goal is to delay that rollover until the barrel has gone through the ball. Forcing the position long after contact is listed as a mistake in both the Direction and Top-Hand notes.

What you want at contact:
- Top palm up.
- Bottom palm down.
- Arms connected to the body.
- Space between the forearms (the "money gap").
- The barrel moving toward your target.

---

Q90. What exit velocity and launch angle give a ball home run potential?

A. The note's example: about 100 mph exit velocity with a launch angle near 25 degrees has strong home run potential. It won't leave every park, but it's the combination hitters chase.

Exit velocity is the ball's speed off the bat, in miles per hour. Launch angle is the ball's vertical angle off the bat, in degrees. General ranges:
- Below 0 degrees: driven into the ground.
- 0-10 degrees: ground ball or low line drive.
- 10-20 degrees: line drive.
- 20-30 degrees: strong fly ball or home run with enough exit velocity.
- Very high: easy fly balls or pop-ups.

Results also depend on field size, weather, altitude, and direction. For many hitters, hard line drives near 15 degrees are a more useful goal than lifting every ball to 20-25. The notes don't give a high school exit velocity target.

---

Q91. Can I create backspin by hitting down on the ball?

A. You can, but it doesn't help. A downswing that clips the ball creates too much spin, and the ball tends to go straight up like a pop-up.

Useful backspin comes from the barrel traveling through the ball and meeting it slightly below center. Clip too far underneath and you get a high, weak pop-up with poor exit velocity. Depending on contact, a steep downswing can also give a ground ball or a mishit.

The note compares it to pitchers: they want lots of spin on a four-seam fastball because it makes the pitch drop less. Hitters shouldn't chase spin. Square the ball with an efficient barrel path.

---

Q92. Why does a four-seam fastball look like it rises?

A. It doesn't really rise. Its backspin makes it drop less than a lower-spin pitch, so it finishes higher than your eyes expect. The ball isn't moving up against gravity. It's falling less.

That's why pitchers want high spin on four-seamers.

For hitters, the lesson is not to answer that spin by chopping down on the ball, which only creates excessive spin and pop-ups. High pitches call for a flatter shoulder turn and barrel path.

---

Q93. Why does the Roll drill use a basketball?

A. The basketball's size gives instant feedback. You can feel when your hands or back elbow move out of position.

Setup: a basketball or another large, light ball. Comfortable stance. Hold the ball near the rear shoulder and hand area with the rear elbow comfortably raised, not jammed against your side. First reps slow and with no bat. Don't squeeze the ball hard.

You're probably pushing or disconnecting if:
- The ball moves away from the rear shoulder during the stride.
- Your hands travel toward the pitcher before landing.
- The rear elbow collapses too early.
- The arms move without the torso.
- You lose balance as you rotate.

If that happens, slow down and land with the ball near the rear shoulder before you turn. Reference video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xjtune7wX5s at 2:10 (Gradum Gswing).

---

Q94. What's the difference between a 'V' bat path and a 'Nike swoosh' bat path?

A. A V path comes from a hand push. The hands go straight toward the pitcher before the barrel has turned. The bat dives steeply, cuts across the pitch's path, and stays in the hitting zone only briefly.

A Nike swoosh path comes from a smooth barrel turn. The barrel enters the pitch's plane earlier, travels through the contact area, and leaves more room for timing adjustments.

The cue: "Turn the barrel first; do not shove the hands toward the ball."

---

Q95. When my front foot lands, should my swing already be underway?

A. No. Landing puts you in an adjustable launch position. The swing hasn't started.

At landing:
- You're balanced.
- Your hands are still near the rear shoulder.
- The rear elbow is organized.
- The rear hip still feels loaded.
- You can still delay or stop the swing.

Once you decide to swing, the lower body and torso rotate and the rear elbow works down and around as the barrel turns into the slot. That's "the roll."

This is what keeps you adjustable. If you expected a fastball and get a changeup, your hands haven't fired yet. You can delay the final barrel turn, stay balanced, and deliver the bat on plane.

---

Q96. Why is the lead hand compared to a steering wheel?

A. A powerful engine is useless if the steering can't keep the car on the road. Bat speed is the engine. The lead hand is the steering.

The lead hand is the hand closest to the pitcher, normally the bottom hand: the left hand for a right-handed hitter. It guides the barrel and controls its direction through the zone. If the lead hand and arm pull the barrel off the right path, all that speed won't give you consistent contact.

The lead arm's jobs:
- Keep the hands connected to the torso.
- Guide the barrel into the pitch's plane.
- Control the swing's direction.
- Keep the hands from leaving the body early.
- Stay inside the ball.
- Finish toward the intended field.

It guides. It shouldn't drag or push the bat toward the ball on its own, and it shouldn't be rigid.

---

Q97. What goes wrong when I push both hands toward the pitcher right after landing?

A. The note lays out the bad sequence:
1. The front foot lands.
2. The hands disconnect from the torso.
3. The lead arm pushes forward.
4. The barrel stays behind the hands.
5. You try to recover by forcing the barrel toward the ball.

That creates a narrow, inefficient path. The barrel can cut across the pitch, arrive late, and leave early. Results: weak ground balls, pull-side rollovers, hooked fouls, less bat speed, inconsistent contact, and trouble with off-speed pitches.

The better sequence: load the rear hip, keep the hands near the rear shoulder, land balanced, rotate with the lower body and torso, let the rear shoulder and elbow turn the barrel into the slot, keep the lead hand with the body's rotation, guide the barrel through the ball, then release it naturally.

---

Q98. What does 'the way you come in is the way you go out' mean?

A. It means the barrel's entry path sets its exit path.

If the barrel enters the hitting zone steeply or cuts across the pitch, it usually leaves the same way. That gives you a short contact window.

If the barrel enters behind the ball and matches the pitch's plane, it stays in the zone longer. A good entry path creates a better exit path.

That's why the curveball note pairs it with "short into the zone, long through it" and warns against chopping down at breaking balls.

---

Q99. What is the breaking-ball recognition progression?

A. It's a six-step plan to train your eyes and swing decisions alongside your mechanics:
1. Watch breaking balls without swinging.
2. Call out the pitch type or direction as early as you can.
3. Move to controlled front toss with predictable movement.
4. Alternate fastballs and breaking balls.
5. Swing only at pitches that finish in your intended hitting zone.
6. Practice taking pitches that finish below or outside the zone.

The note's point is that recognition and swing decisions need training, not only the swing itself.

---

Q100. Should I bend my whole body down to reach a breaking ball that's dropping?

A. No. Follow the ball with your eyes while keeping your head stable and your posture balanced. Use your legs, hip hinge, shoulder angle, and barrel path to reach the lower pitch.

For a downward-breaking pitch you may need:
- A stronger hip hinge.
- More shoulder tilt.
- A lower barrel entry point.
- A controlled low-to-high path through contact.
- A bit more time before releasing the barrel.

Don't create a big uppercut, and don't use your arms to chase the ball. If it finishes below the strike zone, take it.

---

Q101. What do supination and pronation mean for my top hand?

A. Pronation is the forearm rotating so the palm turns down. Supination is the palm facing up.

For the top hand (the right hand for a right-handed hitter), early pronation is the problem. The palm turns down before or during contact, usually from hammering down to "get on top" of the ball. The barrel then travels steeply across the pitch. Results include topped and weak pull-side grounders, rollovers, hooked fouls, less carry, and trouble covering an outside slider.

What you want is supination through contact: the top palm up as if carrying a pizza, the bottom palm down, and space between the forearms. After contact the forearms turn over naturally. The goal is to delay the rollover, not to stop the bat's natural release.

---

Q102. Does moving my top hand away from my shoulder give me more bat speed?

A. It can feel faster, but uncontrolled hand speed doesn't guarantee useful bat speed.

When the hands leave the torso too early:
- The barrel moves off its intended track.
- The arms work separately from the body.
- You lose control of the barrel.
- The swing becomes hard to adjust.
- The barrel may travel around the ball instead of through it.

Results include pop-ups, fouls, swings and misses, inconsistent contact, weak contact off the end of the bat, and trouble with breaking balls and changeups.

Keep the top hand connected near the rear shoulder through the load and stride. Then roll the barrel into the slot as the body turns.

Q103. How did Nathan Eovaldi get Christian Walker out in the 2023 World Series?

A. The setting: Arizona trailed three games to one. In the third, runners were on second and third with nobody out. Walker had a strong season (120 wRC+, 33 home runs) but times fastballs fully, is weaker on breaking balls, and was weak below the zone.

- Pitch 1: a curveball off the plate away.
- Pitch 2: a splitter that missed badly. 2-0.
- Pitch 3: Texas challenged. Walker knew a fastball was coming. It arrived perfectly located down, and he did nothing with it. 2-1.
- Pitch 4: the catcher wouldn't go back to a breaking ball after that splitter, so he changed height. Walker swung expecting a high fastball and got a cutter sliding just off it. 2-2.
- Pitch 5: a 96 mph four-seamer on the outside corner. With the splitter still in his memory, Walker couldn't time it.

The next hitter was retired weakly, and Texas won. The battery won the sequence, not one single pitch.

Q104. If a hitter has only one cold zone, how do pitchers set him up?

A. A battery can't throw all three strikes to one spot, and no hitter sits still while every pitch comes on the same course. So the pitcher needs setup pitches.

Using Shohei Ohtani (hot almost everywhere, cold low and away), the note lists options:
- Finish him low and away.
- Use low and away to win the count, then finish inside.
- Throw to his favorite location but jam it to get a foul.

Pitch type layers on top. Fastball in, curveball away. Or a slider nudged over the middle for a strike, a pitch inside to move his eyes, then back outside for the out.

The hitter's base answer: time the fastball and don't miss the pitch in your own zone. Better hitters can also hunt the pitch in their cold zone when they know it's coming.

---

Q105. What is the Angle Toss drill?

A. Angle Toss trains taking the inside pitch to the opposite field.

Setup: for a right-handed hitter, the feeder tosses from the third-base side, so the ball comes in from the pull-side angle. That's the opposite of the usual first-base-side toss. Left-handed hitters reverse it.

Because of the angle, you can't get the barrel out front and still square the ball. You have to let it travel, stay inside it, and drive it the other way.

It's the foundation of the off-speed approach. Once you can catch a pitch deep and drive it opposite field, the same swing covers an off-speed pitch caught deep. Run it early in the session, before the Bosu ball and V drills.

---

Q106. What is the V drill, and why do I open my top hand?

A. The V drill trains away the rollover on off-speed pitches and builds backspin when you're out in front.

Setup: for a right-handed hitter, the lead arm holds the bat and the top hand opens into a V, palm up, off the handle.

Why: a rollover on an off-speed pitch happens when the back shoulder stops rotating and the hands take over to finish the swing. Opening the top hand removes the hands as an option. The lead arm has to drive the barrel through the zone, and the back shoulder has to keep rotating.

Finishing palm up produces backspin instead of topspin. That turns an early swing on a breaking ball into a liner over the shortstop, a gap double, or a pull-side homer instead of a grounder to third. Run it last, after Angle Toss and the Bosu ball work.

---

Q107. How does the Bosu ball drill help against a good breaking ball at the bottom of the zone?

A. It trains the lower half to get down to the ball instead of reaching for it with the hands. Working off a Bosu ball gives you the feel of the inside of the back knee driving down toward the ball. The legs get you to the ball's depth.

On off-speed pitches, don't disconnect the bat's shaft from your back shoulder. When the hands leave to go get the ball, you're chasing it. That's how good breaking balls and outer-third pitches produce weak contact.

A hanging breaking ball in the middle of the zone doesn't need this move. The drill is built for the good breaker that finishes at the bottom of the zone or below.

The Episode 3 version for outside pitches places a BOSU trainer between your legs as a guide (you don't stand on it), with a tee set low and outside.

---

Q108. How did Cody Bellinger's numbers change when he moved from power to contact?

A. Batting average went .165 (2021), .210 (2022), .305 (2023). Underneath it:
- Strikeout rate: from about 27% back to about 17%, his MVP-year level.
- In-zone whiff rate: 22.4% down to 17.8%.
- Off-speed whiff rate: roughly halved, 31.4% to 15.3%, with a .412 average against off-speed.
- Fastball average: about .303.
- Average exit velocity: 89.4 mph down to 86.9 mph.
- Line-drive rate: 22.2% up to 26.6%.

The exit velocity drop is the price of the trade. He stopped hunting home runs and hunted contact. Better off-speed handling drove most of the rebound. His changes were choking up about two fingers and laying the barrel flatter at the trigger.

Q109. Why is an in-line back knee stronger than one turned inward?

A. The note uses a jump. To jump off one leg, you line the knee up straight over the ground. Nobody jumps with the knee caved inward. Hitting redirects that same energy forward, so the straighter, in-line back leg produces more force. It's hard to find an MLB hitter who doesn't set the back leg in line.

Jung-Hoo Lee's original form turned the knee inward. That's the traditional Japanese form, and it favors precision over force. He turns in place on rotation alone, keeps a deep contact point, and hits compactly. That's great for contact and off-speed pitches. It's harder against premium velocity.

---

Q110. Why did Jung-Hoo Lee lower his hands and set the bat more upright?

A. That hand position is the point his bat exits from. Pre-setting the hands there means he doesn't have to move them during the stretch.

It was one of four changes he tried:
1. Back knee straightened toward an in-line position instead of turned inward.
2. Weight transfer added, instead of turning in place on rotation alone.
3. Hands lower, with a more upright bat.
4. A narrower open stance with the shoulders opened slightly more, to gather force faster.

The last change cost him. His old closed front shoulder was the main reason it never flew open. Relaxing it let the shoulder open occasionally, which is likely why he reverted.

---

Q111. How does Luis Arraez hit for such a high average with weak exit velocity?

A. His contact quality ranks near the bottom of MLB: hard-hit rate in the bottom 2%, barrel rate bottom 5%, average exit velocity bottom 33%. Yet his batting average is near the top, about .323 over five seasons, with a 7.8% strikeout rate.

He hits the ball exactly far enough, over and over. He isn't hitting with his own force. He uses the ball's rebound and puts the bat on it with only enough strength not to be overpowered. That produces lots of balls that lose speed fast and drop once they clear the infield, plus balls his bat control threads down the lines.

He also makes contact on 89.5% of pitches outside the zone and 95.1% inside it, and sprays the ball to every field.

---

Q112. Why is Luis Arraez's swing compared to a boxing jab?

A. A straight punch swings from distance. A jab is a short step and a short thrust. Arraez's swing is the jab.

His rotation is controlled. From standing to the power position, his hands barely move. The front leg going forward creates the energy, and he builds no upper-body coil. The force comes from weight shift.

Compare Freddie Freeman, another line-drive hitter. Freeman's back elbow sweeps a big radius, which shows how much his body rotates. Arraez's elbow moves only a short distance.

Never using extra force or coil is what amplifies his real strengths: bat control and contact.

---

Q113. Does Masataka Yoshida swing with an uppercut?

A. No. He has the slight upward path of a barrel hitter, but nothing extreme. By current standards it's textbook.

He takes a short takeback and comes forward without changing the path of his arms. That clean in-and-out route is magnified by a torso-driven swing. He's short with short arms, but his core strength lets him swing very compactly.

At contact his arms extend toward center field and he usually releases the top hand to lengthen the swing arc. His bat control lives in his front hand.

---

Q114. What three things carry Corey Seager's swing?

A. 1. Hands. He times every plate appearance to the fastball, so his hands sit near the chest. That's where they can be drawn fastest and where a person naturally produces force.

2. Center of gravity. Ordinary stance and stride width, but an aggressive stride. The swing starts with a tap of the front foot, not a high leg lift. The front leg strides far forward while the weight doesn't leak with it. From that power position the body unwinds: knee, pelvis, torso, shoulder.

3. Barrel. The analyst calls it the completion of any good swing, since the ball is struck by the bat, not the arm. The bat face has to meet the roughly 5-degree angle most pitches present at the front of the plate. So the barrel comes around wide instead of stabbing in.

---

Q115. How much weight does Giancarlo Stanton keep back on his long stride?

A. Up to 60%. With a stride that wide, you'd expect his weight to carry forward, but he leaves as much as 60% behind.

That costs him some weight transfer. In return, his front leg brakes firmly and his back foot does a slight scissoring action. Both thicken the force instead.

The rest of his setup: a closed stance, a front-foot timing tap, a toe step, then he "tears into it." The coil is small, the stride makes up for it, and the path is nearly flat. The analyst's image is a heavy polearm rather than a blade: thick and solid, not sharp and quick.

---

Q116. Why has Giancarlo Stanton's swing lost some of its spring?

A. The note calls it elasticity. Comparing 2017 with 2022, his body looks visibly less springy. For a ball player, how much elastic energy the body can produce matters as much as strength.

Whether it's age or a body stiffened by heavy lifting, his home run clips look harder and less fluid. That stiffness plausibly makes adjusting harder and his reactions later.

His swing also doesn't change with two strikes. That explains a 28% strikeout rate that season, against about 22% for Pete Alonso and 24% for Max Muncy. His career on-base percentage of about .353 and steady home runs have carried it.

---

Q117. How did Gunnar Henderson's numbers against breaking balls change after he widened his stride?

A. He widened his stride and weight transfer by about one foot width. That gave him a sturdier base and a wider timing window.

- Against breaking balls: .237 average / .479 slugging / .330 wOBA became .333 / .870 / .537.
- Against off-speed: .241 / .483 / .307 became .286 / .612 / .573.
- He hit 21 home runs.

The catch: timing set for spin makes him slightly late on fastballs, especially in unfavorable counts. Against fastballs he fell from a .250 average to .194, his whiff rate rose from 27.2% to 29.1%, and his strikeout rate from 26% to 32.1%.

Slugging percentage is total bases per at-bat. wOBA (weighted on-base average) credits each way of reaching base by its run value.

---

Q118. What is isolated power, and how did Gunnar Henderson's rookie number compare to the league?

A. Isolated power (ISO) measures extra-base power. It's slugging percentage minus batting average, so singles don't count.

Henderson's 2023 rookie season: 28 home runs, a .489 slugging percentage, and a .234 ISO, roughly 41% above the league average of .166. He hit .255 with a .325 on-base percentage.

The ISO is what flagged his ceiling. Scouts projected 30-30 seasons and an OPS in the .900s built on power and on-base skill. His speed backs it up: 28.7 feet per second sprint speed (top 13%) and 10 steals.

Q119. Why does Kyle Schwarber guess pitches instead of reacting?

A. He has enormous raw strength but weaker precision and bat control. For that kind of hitter, holding several possibilities open in the box is a fast route to an out. So he picks a location and pitch type before the at-bat and plays that game.

He times everything to the fastball, which pitchers throw most, and his fastball production is strong.

The cost is predictable. He's vulnerable to breaking balls, especially sliders that tunnel off the fastball, and to off-speed pitches for the same reason. Add aggressive swings from the first pitch and he sits in the bottom 17% in whiff rate. He walks, strikes out, or homers.

---

Q120. What mechanics let Kyle Schwarber hit the ball so hard?

A. Three features stand out:
- A squat-like posture after the stride. It's built for force, not elegance.
- The rear elbow raised to nearly a right angle, then folded hard. Almost every home run hitter does some version of this to use the full range of motion.
- A long barrel path. The bat head sweeps from behind the head, down along the back, and comes around from far away. The analyst calls this the single most important element in producing a good batted ball.

He also sets his contact point about one ball's width farther out front than normal and pulls about 60% of his batted balls.

---

Q121. According to the Max Muncy note, what two skills does a power hitter need to survive?

A. Contact and pitch judgment.

Raw power isn't rare. Hitters who turn it into results are. Without contact, the power never shows up, and a low enough average drags everything down.

Pitch judgment means deciding which pitch you can punish and waiting for it, like a sniper, instead of reacting to everything. Sluggers who can't wait get taken apart by pitches off the plate.

The rule: a hitter with real power needs only one of the two to survive. Hitters with both, like Judge, Trout, and Alvarez, run the league. Muncy survives on judgment: a .231 career average but a .357 on-base percentage.

Q122. What is wRC+, and how does Mike Trout's compare to the all-time greats?

A. wRC+ (Weighted Runs Created Plus) is an adjusted measure of how many runs a hitter creates. The note calls it the most trusted sabermetric measure of hitting.

The scale: 100 is average, 115 above average, 140 great, 160 excellent, and anything past 170 means the hitter owned the league that season. Because it's relative to league average, 100 means average for whatever league you play in.

Trout's career wRC+ is 172. Only five hitters in history are above him: Babe Ruth (197), Ted Williams (188), and Lou Gehrig, Rogers Hornsby, and Barry Bonds (173). An era adjustment narrows the gap further, since Trout faces the highest average fastball velocity of anyone on that list.

Q123. What makes Mike Trout a five-tool player?

A. He combines all five tools: hitting for average (near .300), power (30-plus homers a year), speed (20-plus steals), arm strength, and solid outfield defense. His typical line has slugging around .600 and an OPS between .980 and 1.000.

Even his weakest year by WAR, 2017, had 33 home runs in about 500 plate appearances, a 1.071 OPS, and a 180 wRC+. He finished fourth in MVP voting.

Before 2022 he swung close to level, waited for his zone and his pitch, and drove the ball gap to gap rather than hunting pure home runs.

Q124. Why did Seiya Suzuki struggle in his first MLB season?

A. Pitchers attack an unfamiliar hitter with fastballs down the middle and only work the corners once data says he can hit. Suzuki found MLB velocity harder than expected.

The drop-off:
- 2021 in Japan: .317, 38 home runs, 1.073 OPS, 189 wRC+.
- 2022 in Chicago: .260s, 14 home runs, .769 OPS, 117 wRC+.
- 113 strikeouts at a 24% rate, a level he never approached in Japan.
- 89.6 mph average exit velocity, which ranked in the middle of the league.

Q125. What is a toe step, and how does Shohei Ohtani use it?

A. A toe step is a small timing move: the front foot steps forward lightly onto the toe instead of a big leg lift.

In Ohtani's current swing, the only difference from his earlier form is that the front leg is planted forward. His hands stretch only as far as the front leg moves. Nothing pulls further back and nothing drops. From the stance, when the ball comes, the swing simply goes.

Then the power comes from the lower body: the pelvis folds, the legs settle slightly, and the front knee turns outward to start rotation. Giancarlo Stanton also uses a toe step after his front-foot timing tap.

Q126. What did Shohei Ohtani improve to hit for both average and power?

Apart from down-and-in, no weakness shows anywhere in his zone. That breadth of coverage is likely why his batting average climbed to .300 alongside the home runs.

Q129. How open was Aaron Judge's stance before and after his change?

Q131. What is Ronald Acuña Jr.'s 40-70 season, and why is it so rare?

Q132. How can I hit the ball with more power?

A. The notes put most of your power in what happens before contact, not in the follow-through.

1. Load the rear hip. Hinge at the hips so the rear glute and hamstring engage. That's where rotational power comes from. Avoid standing tall and pushing the knees forward.
2. Build separation. Let the lower body move toward the pitcher while the upper body stays loaded. That twist creates torque (rotational force).
3. Rotate from the ground up: foot, knee, hip, then upper body. The bat swings out last.
4. Keep the hands connected. Uncontrolled hand speed isn't useful bat speed.
5. Square the ball on a slightly upward path.

Power hitters in the notes also use a big rear-elbow range of motion and a long barrel path (Kyle Schwarber). Raw power only counts if you also make contact or choose the right pitches (Max Muncy note).

Q133. Why do I keep topping the ball?

A. Topping the ball means the barrel meets it above center, usually from a downward swing.

Common causes:
- Chopping down or trying to "get on top of the ball." A negative attack angle crosses the pitch's path briefly and tends to make topped grounders and rollovers.
- Turning the top palm down too early, which makes the barrel steep.
- Pushing the hands at the ball before the barrel turns.

Fix it by matching the pitch's plane:
1. Load into the rear hip.
2. Land balanced and adjustable.
3. Turn the barrel behind the ball.
4. Enter the pitch's plane early.
5. Move the barrel forward and slightly up through the zone.
6. Make solid contact near the center of the ball.

Keep the top palm up through contact. Don't overcorrect by scooping or leaning back.

---

Q134. How can I cut down on my strikeouts?

A. The notes show three ways hitters did it.

1. Trade some power for contact. Cody Bellinger choked up about two fingers and laid his bat flatter at the trigger. His strikeout rate fell from about 27% to 17%, and his off-speed whiff rate was cut in half. His exit velocity dropped a little. That's the trade.

2. Swing less and hunt one pitch. Pete Crow-Armstrong took Alex Bregman's advice. His swing rate fell from 60% to 49% and his chase rate from 41.7% to 34.1%.

3. Own a zone. Kyle Schwarber ignores pitches outside the zone he picked and only expands with two strikes.

Your swing path matters too. A steep path is in the zone for an instant, which means more misses. An on-plane barrel stays in the zone longer.

For scale, Luis Arraez strikes out 7.8% of the time. The notes don't give a high school strikeout target.

---

Q135. What should I change in my approach with two strikes?

A. The notes don't lay out a full two-strike approach. They do show what happens with and without an adjustment.

- Giancarlo Stanton's max-effort swing doesn't change with two strikes. A high strikeout rate (28% that season) and low contact rate follow.
- Kyle Schwarber only releases his self-defined zone after two strikes. Before that he takes pitches that aren't his, even strikes.
- Gunnar Henderson's timing set for breaking balls made him late on fastballs in unfavorable counts.
- Cody Bellinger's choke-up for contact shows how giving up a little power buys contact.

From those: with two strikes, widen what you'll swing at, stay on time for the fastball, and consider a more contact-oriented swing.

---

Q136. How do I hit a slider?

A. A slider moves mostly sideways with some downward action. Handle it like other breaking balls.

- Recognize it. Learn when the pitcher throws it, how it looks out of the hand, and where it finishes. Attack the hittable ones and take the ones that finish off the plate.
- Don't commit early. Land balanced with your hands still near the rear shoulder.
- Get on plane. Against a slider moving down and away, a chopping swing loses coverage fast. An on-plane barrel rolled into the slot keeps covering the ball's movement and can drive it to the opposite-field gap.
- Don't pull your front shoulder away. Let the back shoulder work toward the pitch.
- Keep the top palm up through contact. Turning it down early makes an outside slider hard to cover.

---

Q137. How do I hit a changeup?

A. A changeup is slower than the fastball and is built to make you early. Two things help most.

1. Stop pulling every fastball. If your contact point is always way out front, there's nothing left for a slower pitch. Learn to drive the inside fastball to the opposite field (the Angle Toss drill). Then a changeup caught deep goes the other way, one caught off the front foot goes up the middle, and one caught early goes to the pull side. Every timing outcome has a destination.

2. Create separation. Stride with the lower body while the hands stay near the rear shoulder, and land without committing them. When the changeup comes, you can delay the final barrel turn and stay on plane.

An on-plane barrel also stays in the zone longer, so a pitch that arrives late can still be squared.

---

Q138. Pitchers throw harder than I'm used to. How do I catch up to velocity?

A. Don't let hard pitches travel deep. At 95-98 mph, waiting until the ball reaches the catcher's side of the plate makes you late. Make contact near the plane of your front foot.

Get ready sooner. Seiya Suzuki's adjustment to MLB velocity was:
- An earlier contact point, a beat or two sooner.
- A shorter stride, which costs some coil but buys a step of preparation.
- Hands starting about a full tempo earlier.

Corey Seager times every at-bat to the fastball and sets his hands near his chest, where they can be drawn fastest.

Earlier timing isn't the same as reaching far out front. Hitting inside pitches way out front hooks them foul. Jung-Hoo Lee's note shows the other side: a deep contact point helps contact but struggles against premium velocity.

---

Q139. Should my batting stance be open, closed, or square?

A. The notes say there's no single right answer.

- Aaron Judge hit 62 home runs from an open stance, about 20 degrees open. He moved to about 5 degrees because outside pitches felt far away and he didn't always feel square after his leg lift. The note says open versus square isn't the real question.
- Jung-Hoo Lee's open stance with a fully closed front shoulder kept his shoulder from flying open. When he opened it more, the shoulder sometimes came open, and he reverted.
- Giancarlo Stanton hits from a closed stance.

Pick the stance where you feel balanced, end up square to the pitcher when you land, and can cover the outside pitch.

---

Q140. How long should my stride be?

A. The notes show tradeoffs, not one right length.

- Longer: Gunnar Henderson widened his stride by about one foot width. He got a sturdier base, a wider timing window, and much better results on breaking balls, but became slightly late on fastballs.
- Shorter: Seiya Suzuki shortened his stride. It cost some coil and weight transfer but bought a step of preparation against MLB velocity.
- Max Muncy uses a short stride, gathers energy behind him, then pushes forward with his head still.
- Corey Seager and Giancarlo Stanton stride far forward without letting the weight leak with it. Stanton keeps up to 60% back.

Whatever length you use, land balanced with your hands still back.

---

Q141. Should I use a leg kick or a toe tap?

A. Either can work. The trigger is the first move that sets your rhythm and catches the pitch's timing. Hitters wiggle the bat, tap a foot, or use a big leg kick.

What matters is the flow after it, from the ground up: knee, then hip, then torso and shoulders, into the power position.

Examples:
- Corey Seager uses a tap of the front foot instead of a high leg lift.
- Shohei Ohtani uses a toe step with almost no extra movement.
- Giancarlo Stanton taps, toe steps, then swings.
- Aaron Judge uses a leg lift.

Choose the one that lets you land balanced and on time.

---

Q142. Where should my hands be in my stance?

A. Near the rear shoulder and connected to the body is the common thread. The top hand should stay organized near the rear shoulder through the load and stride, without being pressed rigidly against it.

Variations from the notes:
- Corey Seager sets his hands near his chest because he times every pitch to the fastball, and that's where hands can be drawn fastest.
- Jung-Hoo Lee lowered his hands with a more upright bat to pre-set the spot his bat exits from.

Whatever you choose, don't drop the hands or barrel under the pitch during your load, and don't push them toward the pitcher after you land.

---

Q143. Should I keep my weight back or shift it forward?

A. Both styles work.

The Basic Hitting Mechanism note names two ways to deliver energy:
- Active forward weight transfer, like Yordan Alvarez.
- Rotating on the back-leg axis, like Bryce Harper. Aaron Judge and Shohei Ohtani also hit this way, and Juan Soto holds power firmly on his back leg.

Other examples: Ronald Acuña Jr. starts near 50/50 and moves forward. Giancarlo Stanton keeps up to 60% back on a long stride. Luis Arraez gets his force from weight shift.

What every version shares: the weight shouldn't leak forward early, and the head stays still.

---

Q144. How do I keep my head still when I swing?

A. A still head comes from balance and from not lunging or pulling off the ball.

Max Muncy takes a short stride, gathers energy behind him, then pushes forward onto his center axis with his head still. His shoulders stay closed until rotation reaches the upper body. The analyst says that if the head moves, the swing has to be rebuilt from scratch.

Against breaking balls, follow the ball with your eyes while your head stays stable. Don't bend your whole body down with the pitch, and don't let your head drift away when your front shoulder pulls off.

In the load, move the hip back instead of swaying over the back leg. Swaying makes your head drift backward.

---

Q145. Why does my front shoulder fly open too early?

A. The notes connect it to pulling away from the pitch, often on breaking balls moving away or when you're trying to pull.

When the body pulls away:
- The head moves away from the pitch.
- The hands disconnect from the torso.
- The barrel cuts across the ball.
- You lose the outer part of the plate.
- Contact is often weak or off the end of the bat.

Fixes:
- Stay balanced and let the back shoulder work toward the pitch.
- Try the Closed-Net drill: a net about three feet behind you boxes you in and helps keep the front shoulder from opening.
- Keep the shoulders closed until rotation reaches the upper body, like Max Muncy. Jung-Hoo Lee's fully closed front shoulder was the main reason his never flew open.

---

Q146. How do I hit the ball to the opposite field?

A. Change the barrel's direction, not your contact depth. Meet the ball near your front-foot plane and let the barrel work toward the opposite field: right-center for a right-handed hitter.

Drills:
- Angle Toss. The feeder tosses from the pull-side angle, so you have to let the ball travel, stay inside it, and drive it the other way.
- Bosu ball. It trains the legs to get to outer pitches instead of reaching with the hands.

Luis Arraez even trains taking inside pitches the other way, so pitchers have nowhere left to throw.

Avoid reaching with your arms, pulling your front shoulder away, and letting outside fastballs travel too deep.

---

Q147. What should a good round of batting practice look like?

A. Use a whole-field routine. Mike Trout's is the model in the notes:
1. First round: drive balls toward right-center and right field (for a right-handed hitter).
2. Then work through the middle of the field.
3. Occasionally drive one to left-center to check that you're square to the pitcher and balanced.

The aim is to drive inside, middle, and outside pitches to left-center, center, and right-center. Don't copy his swing. Copy the approach.

For new movements, the notes use the same progression: dry swings, then tee, then controlled front toss, then mixed locations. Start slow and add speed only when the movement repeats.

---

Q148. What tee drills can I do on my own?

A. Two tee drills from the notes:

1. T drill (high pitches). Start with the ball mid-zone and raise the tee gradually. Keep your normal load and landing, keep the top hand strong and connected, and preserve barrel lag. Match your back-shoulder angle to the ball's height and drive a firm line.

2. BOSU ball drill (low-away pitches). Set the tee low and outside. Put a BOSU trainer between your legs as a guide (don't stand on it). Load the back hip, land, drive the back knee and hip, let the back shoulder work toward the pitch, and turn the barrel behind the ball toward the middle or opposite field.

The Closed-Net drill also works alone with slow-motion swings. The curveball note suggests starting breaking-ball plane work on a tee before front toss.

---

Q149. Why am I always out in front and pulling everything foul?

A. Usually your contact point is too far out front.

When you reach far out front, especially on inside pitches, the barrel is already turning toward the pull side. For a right-handed hitter the ball hooks foul past third.

Hitters taught to hit inside fastballs out front often end up pulling every fastball. That shrinks the hitting window to one point and leaves nothing for slower pitches.

Fix it by meeting the ball near your front-foot plane and changing the barrel's direction instead. On inside pitches, turn tighter with the hands close to your body and aim for left-center, not the line. Angle Toss trains letting the ball travel.

---

Q150. How do I hit a low pitch?

A. Low pitches need more shoulder tilt and a more noticeable low-to-high barrel path.

- Use your legs and hip hinge to get down. Don't bend your whole body or drop your hands.
- A low-to-forward, slightly upward path lets you get underneath a low pitch and create lift and backspin.
- For low breaking pitches, you may also need a stronger hip hinge, a lower barrel entry, and a bit more time before releasing the barrel.
- It's not a big uppercut.
- Take pitches that finish below the zone.

For low-and-away pitches, the BOSU ball drill trains the back knee, back hip, and rear shoulder to reach them.

---

Q151. Should I try to swing up on the ball?

A. Slightly up, yes. A big uppercut, no.

Pitches come in on a slightly downward path. A barrel moving forward and slightly upward (a positive attack angle) matches that path. It stays in the zone longer and produces hard line drives and fly balls gap to gap.

Don't manufacture it by dropping the back shoulder, lowering your hands, leaning back, or scooping. A very high launch angle can just be a pop-up. The upward path should come from the body's rotation and the barrel's turn.

Adjust for height too. High pitches need a flatter path, and a steep uppercut against a high pitch is a listed mistake.

---

Q152. Is it better to hit line drives or fly balls?

A. The Launch Angle note leans toward hard line drives for most hitters.

A launch angle near 15 degrees can produce a hard line drive. For many hitters, consistently hitting hard liners is a more useful goal than lifting every ball to 20-25 degrees.

Fly balls at 20-30 degrees can become home runs only with enough exit velocity, for example about 100 mph at about 25 degrees. Very high angles just become easy outs.

Judge launch angle together with exit velocity, contact quality, direction, pitch location, and your own strengths. The goal is hard, useful contact.

Mike Trout's move to more fly balls added home runs but cost him contact and walks.

---

Q153. How can I increase my bat speed?

A. The notes don't give a bat speed program or target numbers. Bat speed is how fast the barrel moves, in miles per hour, and the notes don't say what's good for a high school hitter.

What they do say: speed comes from the body, not the hands alone.
- Moving the top hand away from the shoulder can feel fast, but uncontrolled hand speed doesn't guarantee useful bat speed.
- Pushing the hands at the pitcher actually reduces bat speed.

The sequence that produces it: load the rear hip, stay connected, rotate from the ground up, let the barrel lag briefly, then accelerate it through the zone without an isolated wrist flick.

Power hitters use a big rear-elbow range of motion and a long barrel path. And bat speed only helps if the lead hand keeps it on the right path.

---

Q154. How do I become a better contact hitter?

A. The contact hitter notes show several routes.

- Control the barrel. Cody Bellinger choked up about two fingers and laid his bat flatter at the trigger. His strikeout rate fell from about 27% to 17%.
- Keep the swing compact. Luis Arraez's swing is a jab: his hands barely move and he builds no extra coil. Masataka Yoshida takes a short takeback with a slight upward path.
- Use the whole field. Arraez sprays the ball (38.9% to center, 32.6% to left, 28.5% to right) and even takes inside pitches the other way.
- Trust the front hand. Yoshida's bat control lives in his front hand.
- Keep the lower-body move short. Jung-Hoo Lee's inward back knee lets him turn in place, keep a deep contact point, and hit compactly. It favors precision over force and struggles against premium velocity.

The usual cost is some exit velocity. Arraez ranks near the bottom in hard-hit rate but near the top in batting average.

---

Q155. Should I guess what pitch is coming or just react?

A. It depends on your strengths.

Kyle Schwarber guesses. He has big strength but weaker bat control, so holding several possibilities open is a fast route to an out. He picks a location and pitch type before the at-bat. The cost is vulnerability to sliders and off-speed pitches.

The default from the Batter's Mental Game note: time the fastball, and don't miss the pitch in your own location.

Pete Crow-Armstrong improved by hunting one pitch per at-bat instead of attacking everything.

Whichever you do, have a plan. David Ortiz's rule is to die with your plan rather than guess randomly.

---

Q156. How do I stop chasing pitches out of the zone?

A. The notes give a few tools.

- Swing less. Alex Bregman's advice cut Pete Crow-Armstrong's chase rate from 41.7% to 34.1%, and his walk rate rose from 4.5% to 12%.
- Train recognition. Watch breaking balls without swinging, call the pitch early, alternate fastballs and breakers, swing only at pitches that finish in your zone, and practice taking ones that finish below or outside it.
- Know your ceiling. Avoid high fastballs above about belly-button height.
- Own a zone. Kyle Schwarber ignores anything outside the zone he set for himself until two strikes.
- Know the pitcher's best pitch and where it usually finishes.

---

Q157. What should I be thinking about in the batter's box?

A. Have a plan and stick to it. David Ortiz and Manny Ramirez planned every at-bat. If they failed, they wanted to fail executing the plan, not guessing.

Prepare the way the Batter's Mental Game note describes:
- What does this pitcher throw?
- What does he finish with?
- What's worth hunting?
- How do I cover my weakness?

The base default: time the fastball, and don't miss the pitch in your own location.

Keep the count in mind. The pitcher-hitter guessing game lives in the count battle.

---

Q158. How do I bounce back from a bad at-bat or a slump?

A. Start with perspective. Baseball is a game of failure, and failing seven out of ten times still makes you great. Manny Ramirez didn't care about an early strikeout. He knew he'd get the pitcher later, or the next day. Ortiz calls the drive to deal with adversity the "15%" that analytics can't give you.

Then make a concrete change. Pete Crow-Armstrong went about 70 games hitting .195. Offseason breathing work, mind control, and mirror drills moved nothing. What worked was a real approach change: swinging less, hunting one pitch per at-bat, and standing closer to the plate.

Pair the mindset with a specific adjustment.

---

Q159. Why does the count matter so much for a hitter?

A. The count is where the pitcher-hitter guessing game lives. Three strikes make an out, four balls put a runner on, and all the calculation hides in that frame.

Example from the 2023 World Series: at 2-0, the classic hitter's count, Nathan Eovaldi had to throw Christian Walker a fastball, and Walker knew it. It came perfectly located down and he did nothing with it. Texas then won the at-bat by changing height and pitch type.

The lesson: in a hitter's count, be ready to punish the pitch you know is coming. The pitcher is sequencing over the whole count, so you should be thinking ahead too.

---

Q160. Why do I hit so many foul balls?

A. Common causes from the notes:
- Contact too far out front on inside pitches. The barrel is already turning to the pull side, so the ball hooks foul.
- Letting outside fastballs travel too deep. You end up late and foul them straight back.
- Rolling the top hand over early. That produces sharply hooked fouls.
- Disconnecting the hands. The barrel goes around the ball instead of through it.
- Swinging horizontally around your body (east-to-west). The barrel cuts across the zone and hooks the ball.

Fixes: use one contact window near your front foot, keep your hands connected, keep the top palm up through contact, and use a south-to-north (Ferris-wheel) path.

---

Q161. Why do I swing and miss at pitches I should be able to hit?

A. Often your barrel is in the hitting zone for too short a time. A steep path crosses the pitch's path for only an instant, so any small timing miss becomes a whiff.

Causes:
- Chopping down or trying to "get on top."
- Driving the knob straight at the ball.
- Moving the hands first.
- Disconnecting the hands from the body.
- Letting outside fastballs travel too deep.

Fix: get on plane early. Load the rear shoulder blade, roll the barrel into the slot, let it lag briefly, then accelerate through the pitch. The Bat Path note lists fewer swings and misses as one result.

---

Q162. What muscles should I feel working in my batting stance?

A. Mainly your rear glute and hamstring.

To engage them, hinge at the hips instead of only bending your knees. Move the hips back with a slight knee bend, as if closing a car door with your rear hip, and keep your back neutral. Hold it briefly and feel the rear glute and hamstring tighten.

If you mostly feel the front of your thighs, you're quad-dominant: hips too tall and knees pushed forward.

Up top, the Bat Path note also loads the rear shoulder blade slightly toward the spine as you stride.

---

Q163. How should I progress a new drill from dry swings to live pitching?

A. The notes repeat the same progression: slow and simple first, then add a moving ball, then mix it up.

Examples:
- Roll drill: basketball with no bat, then dry swings, then tee, then controlled front toss.
- BOSU drill: tee low-away, then front toss low-away, middle-away, high-away.
- PVC pipe drill: tennis-ball toss, then the T drill with a real bat and tee.
- Breaking balls: tee first, then front toss with breaking movement.
- Off-speed drills: order matters. Angle Toss, then Bosu ball, then V drill.
- Drop-Bat drill: start slow and add speed only when you can repeat it safely.

---

Q164. Is batting average the most important stat for a hitter?

A. Not by itself.

Kyle Schwarber hit .201 but had a .349 on-base percentage, with 116 walks against 106 hits and 43 home runs. That made him a defensible leadoff hitter. Max Muncy has a .231 career average but a .357 on-base percentage.

On-base percentage (OBP) is how often you reach base by any means, including walks. The notes call it the most important requirement for a leadoff hitter. The Mike Trout note uses wRC+, an adjusted measure of runs created, as the most trusted measure of hitting.

Average still matters. Luis Arraez wins with it. It's one part of the picture.

---

Q165. What does exit velocity tell me about my hitting?

A. Exit velocity is the ball's speed off the bat, in miles per hour. Hit probability and home run probability both rise with it. The Stanton note calls it the most trustworthy batted-ball indicator. Giancarlo Stanton averages around 93 mph and has been in the top 1% every year since tracking began in 2015.

Pair it with launch angle. About 100 mph at about 25 degrees has strong home run potential.

It isn't everything. Luis Arraez is in the bottom third in exit velocity and still hits over .300.

The notes don't give a high school exit velocity target.

---

Q166. Should I copy a pro hitter's swing?

A. Copy the approach, not the swing.

The Episode 2 note says an amateur shouldn't copy Mike Trout's exact swing. Learn from his approach instead: stay balanced, stay square to the pitcher, and build a path that uses the whole field.

Pro swings also depend on the player. Ronald Acuña Jr.'s mechanics look dated on paper (a 50/50 start and a compact down-swing style), but they work because of his eye, restraint, and elasticity. Jung-Hoo Lee's contact comes from his form combined with his talent, and the note says the form alone doesn't produce that for everyone.

Borrow the shared principles: balance, connection, an on-plane barrel, and a consistent contact window.

---

Q167. What should my back elbow be doing during the swing?

A. - In the stance: comfortably raised, not collapsed against your side.
- At landing: organized behind the body. It shouldn't collapse early.
- In the swing: it works naturally down and around as the barrel turns into the slot. That's "the roll." Don't force it against your body.

Power examples: Kyle Schwarber raises his rear elbow to nearly a right angle, then folds it hard to use his full range of motion. Shohei Ohtani tucks the elbow as tightly as possible while keeping the angle between his hands and forearm.

The common "swing down" feeling some pros describe actually comes from the elbow driving down toward the body.

---

Q168. How do I time the pitcher?

A. Use a trigger to set your rhythm: a bat wiggle, a foot tap, or a leg kick. Then let the movement flow from the ground up into the power position.

Time the fastball by default. Corey Seager times every plate appearance to it, and the Batter's Mental Game note calls that the base of everything.

The hip load buys you a brief balanced moment to track the pitch instead of rushing forward.

To handle slower pitches, keep your hands back when you land. If you haven't committed your hands, you can delay the final barrel turn when you see something off-speed.

---

Q169. Should I swing at the first pitch?

A. Only if it's the pitch you want.

Kyle Schwarber swings without hesitation from the first pitch, regardless of count, when he gets his pitch. He also takes pitches that aren't his, even strikes.

Pete Crow-Armstrong cut his first-pitch swing rate from 53% to 39% as part of swinging less, and his results jumped.

So be selective, not passive. The notes don't give an ideal first-pitch swing rate.

---

Q170. Why don't my fly balls carry?

A. The notes point to a few causes.

- Contact too far below center. You get a high, weak pop-up with poor exit velocity.
- Low exit velocity. Fly balls at 20-30 degrees need enough speed to go anywhere.
- Turning the top palm down early. The Top-Hand note lists reduced carry as one result.
- Chopping down to "create backspin." That makes too much spin and the ball goes straight up.

Useful backspin comes from the barrel traveling through the ball and meeting it slightly below center. The V drill's palm-up finish is meant to produce backspin rather than topspin.

---

Q171. Why do I keep hitting the ball off the end of the bat?

A. The notes give two main causes.

- Pulling your body away from the pitch, especially a breaking ball moving toward the outside. Your head moves, your hands disconnect, and the barrel cuts across the ball. Contact is often weak or off the end.
- Disconnecting your hands from your body to chase bat speed. The barrel leaves its track and contact gets weak off the end.

Reaching for outside pitches with only your arms does the same.

Fixes: let the back shoulder work toward the pitch, keep your hands connected near the rear shoulder, and use your legs (back knee and back hip) to get to outer pitches.

---

Q172. Why do I keep getting jammed on inside pitches?

A. The notes don't use the word "jammed," but they cover the pieces.

Contact near the handle happens when the ball gets too deep on you. On inside pitches, the fix is tighter rotation, not reaching:
- Turn more tightly and keep your hands close to your body.
- Meet the ball near your front-foot plane.
- Let the barrel work through the zone with a slightly upward turn. Don't force your hands inward.
- Stay connected so the barrel comes from behind the ball, which is what "staying inside the ball" means.

The Closed-Net drill trains a compact turn on inside pitches. Don't overcorrect by reaching way out front. That hooks the ball foul.

---

Q173. My coach says I'm lunging at the ball. How do I fix it?

A. Lunging is your body drifting forward too early.

What the notes suggest:
- Load into the rear hip. A good hip load lets you stay balanced for a moment while you track the pitch instead of rushing forward.
- Don't sway. Swaying back over the rear foot forces you to rush forward to recover.
- Create separation. Your lower body moves forward while your upper body stays loaded and your hands stay near the rear shoulder.
- Don't commit your hands early. Early hands on a slower pitch make you reach forward and lose balance.
- Stride without leaking weight. Corey Seager strides far forward, but his weight doesn't go with it.

The Attack Angle note's first step says the same: start balanced, without falling back or lunging forward.

---

Q174. What's the difference between a power hitter and a contact hitter?

A. Luis Arraez and Kyle Schwarber are the two extremes in the notes.

Contact (Arraez): about .323 average, 7.8% strikeouts, bottom-tier exit velocity. He sprays the ball to all fields with a short "jab" swing and gets his force from weight shift.

Power (Schwarber): .201 average with 43 home runs and 116 walks. He pulls about 60% of his batted balls, guesses pitches, and uses a hard elbow fold and a long barrel path.

The Muncy note says a power hitter needs either contact or pitch judgment to survive. Hitters with power plus both skills, like Judge, Trout, and Alvarez, run the league.

---

Q175. Is it bad to hit ground balls?

A. Not automatically. Ground balls can become hits, and every successful hitter hits some.

The problem is repeatedly driving the ball into the ground. That limits extra-base hits and home runs. Chopping down may work against young defenders, but it isn't reliable at higher levels.

If you're piling up weak groundouts or double plays, check your swing path and contact point. The answer isn't always to swing higher. Timing, barrel direction, and where the ball meets the bat matter too.

A low launch angle isn't automatically bad, and a high one isn't automatically good. Hard, useful contact is the goal.

---

Q176. What's the difference between a curveball, a slider, and a splitter?

A. From the notes:
- Curveball: moves downward, and may move sideways too.
- Slider: moves mostly sideways, with some downward action.
- Splitter: looks like a fastball, then drops sharply near the plate.

All three are slower than the fastball. The off-speed category also includes the changeup and the forkball. The pitcher's goal with all of them is to change your timing, not overpower you.

They're hard because they move off the spot your eyes first expect, they arrive slower, and they're often the pitcher's best weapon.

---

Q177. Should I try to pull the ball or go the other way?

A. Both, depending on the pitch. Don't pull every time.

The off-speed note is clear that it's not against pulling the fastball. It's against pulling the fastball every time. The middle of the field, gap to gap, is the most real estate you have.

The Contact Point note matches direction to location for a right-handed hitter: inside pitch to left-center, middle to center, outside to right-center.

Hitters choose differently. Kyle Schwarber pulls about 60% as a home-run strategy and accepts the strikeouts. Luis Arraez sprays everywhere.

---

Q178. How do I adjust my swing for high pitches versus low pitches?

A. Same swing, different plane.

- Low pitch: more shoulder tilt and a more noticeable low-to-high barrel path.
- High pitch: a flatter shoulder turn and a flatter barrel path.

Think of the barrel as an extension of your shoulders. The angle of your back shoulder steers the barrel's direction.

Keep the same balanced load, lower-body movement, and connected rotation. Use your legs and hip hinge to change the plane. Don't reach up with your hands or stand up out of your stance.

The PVC pipe drill and the T drill train this.

---

Q179. How do I beat a pitcher's best pitch?

A. Study it, then take it away.

The curveball note says to learn:
- When the pitch is likely to be thrown.
- How it looks out of the hand.
- Where it usually finishes.
- Which versions to attack and which to take.

Showing you can handle a pitcher's best pitch forces him to use less comfortable options.

The off-speed note treats the inside fastball as the pitcher's main weapon. Take it away by letting it travel and driving it to the opposite field. Then you don't have to cheat for it, and the off-speed pitches lose their power over you.

---

Q180. What does having a 'short swing' actually mean?

A. Short into the zone, long through it.

"Be short to the ball" is useful when it means cutting unnecessary movement. It hurts when it means pushing your hands straight at the ball. That gives the barrel a steep path and a tiny hitting window.

A compact swing includes:
- A controlled load.
- A balanced landing.
- Connected hands.
- An early barrel turn.
- A direct path into the pitch's plane.
- A long path through the contact area.

---

Q181. Should I drop my back shoulder to lift the ball?

A. No. Dropping the back shoulder is on the Attack Angle note's list of wrong ways to chase launch angle. On high pitches, dropping it too much is a listed mistake too.

Shoulder tilt should match pitch height. Low pitches get more tilt. High pitches get less. It shouldn't be a dip to create lift.

Lift should come from an on-plane barrel and squaring the ball, with contact slightly below center.

---

Q182. My hands drop right before I swing. Is that a problem?

A. It can be. Step 2 of the Attack Angle note says to load without dropping the hands or barrel under the anticipated pitch. A positive attack angle should come from the body's rotation and the barrel's turn, not an artificial scoop. "Lowering the hands" is also on its list of ways not to chase launch angle.

Two things are different:
- Setting your hands lower in your stance on purpose. Jung-Hoo Lee did this to pre-set where his bat exits. That's a setup position, not a drop during the load.
- The Drop-Bat drill's "drop." It means letting the barrel settle into its working position under control, not your hands sinking.

---

Q183. What should my follow-through look like?

A. The notes treat the follow-through as an extension of the swing's flow, not a source of power.

What it should do:
- Keep the barrel going in your intended direction. Don't hit the ball and immediately pull the barrel away.
- Accelerate forward through the field, not down toward the ball.
- Let your hands and forearms turn over naturally after contact. Don't force the palm-up position.
- Release the barrel naturally after your body turns toward the pitcher.

Pros vary. Masataka Yoshida extends his arms toward center field and releases his top hand for a longer arc. Shohei Ohtani keeps the angle between his hands and forearm instead of fully extending.

---

Q184. What is hip-shoulder separation, and why does it matter?

A. Separation is your lower body starting toward the pitcher while your upper body stays loaded. That twist between hips and shoulders builds torque, which is rotational force.

During the stride:
- The lower body gains ground.
- The rear hip stays loaded.
- The rear shoulder blade stays engaged.
- The hands stay near the rear shoulder.
- The upper body doesn't drift forward.

It doesn't mean pulling your hands as far back as possible. Too much stretch creates tension and disconnects your arms.

Why it matters: power, plus time. Your hands haven't fired, so you can adjust to off-speed pitches. Giancarlo Stanton shows you can hit with a smaller coil, and Jung-Hoo Lee's note shows too much coil can take too long to gather.

The notes don't give a degree target for separation.

---

Q185. What is the power position, or launch position?

A. It's the loaded, ready-to-swing position you reach after your trigger and stride.

From the Basic Hitting Mechanism note: the trigger starts a bottom-up flow (knee, then hip, then torso and shoulders) that leads into the "launch position" or "power position."

What it looks like:
- Weight and tension loaded in the rear hip, with the rear glute and hamstring engaged.
- Front foot down and balanced.
- Hands still near the rear shoulder, rear elbow organized.
- Still adjustable: you can delay or stop the swing.

Shohei Ohtani builds his by folding the pelvis, settling both legs slightly, and pushing his hips a little forward.

---

Q186. Why do I roll over on changeups and curveballs?

A. The off-speed note's diagnosis: your back shoulder stops rotating, and your hands take over to finish the swing. It adds that reaching with the hands to go get a breaking ball is how good breakers produce weak contact.

Fixes:
- The V drill. Hold the bat with the lead arm and open the top hand palm up off the handle. Your hands can't finish the swing, so the lead arm drives the barrel and the back shoulder keeps turning.
- Finish palm up. That gives backspin instead of topspin, so an early swing becomes a liner instead of a grounder to third.
- Keep the bat's shaft connected to your back shoulder and get to low pitches with your legs.
- Create separation so your hands don't fire before you recognize the pitch. The curveball note says committing your hands before a slower pitch arrives makes you reach forward, lose balance, and roll over. Land with your hands still near the rear shoulder.

Q187. Why is the middle of the field the best place to hit the ball?

A. It's the most real estate. The off-speed note says the middle of the field, from gap to gap, is the largest area available. A hitter who can only pull is competing for the smallest slice.

Staying through the middle also keeps your hitting window big. Every timing outcome has a good destination: late goes the other way, on time goes up the middle, early goes to the pull side.

It's what Mike Trout trains in batting practice, driving inside, middle, and outside pitches to left-center, center, and right-center.

Q188. What happens if I start my swing with my hands?

A. Hands first is the first step of the wrong bat path in the Bat Path note:
1. The hands move first.
2. The top palm starts turning down.
3. The barrel gets steep.
4. The bat cuts across the pitch.
5. The barrel leaves the zone quickly.

The lead-arm note adds what follows: weak grounders, pull-side rollovers, hooked fouls, less bat speed, and trouble with off-speed pitches. The direction note lists "hands moving before the lower body starts rotating" as a mistake.

Start with the lower body and torso. Let the rear elbow and barrel roll into the slot while your hands stay connected.

Q189. How do I use my legs in my swing?

A. The legs load, then start the rotation.

- Load: hinge into the rear hip so the glute and hamstring engage.
- Start: after landing, the foot turns, then the knee, then the hip, then the upper body.
- Ohtani's version: his front knee turns outward to create rotational force, his back knee rotates the same way, and his pelvis turns with them to carry force to contact.
- Reaching low or outside pitches: drive the back knee and back hip toward the hitting area (the BOSU drill). Don't reach with the arms.
- Low breaking balls: let the inside of the back knee drive down so your legs get you to the ball's depth.

Q190. What's the most important part of the swing?

A. The notes stress different things.

- Basic Hitting Mechanism says about 80% of the mechanism happens in the "back space," before the bat reaches the ball: hip hinge, separation, weight transfer.
- The Corey Seager note calls the barrel the completion of any good swing, since the bat hits the ball, not the arm.
- The Kyle Schwarber note calls a long barrel path the single most important element for a good batted ball.

The common idea: build energy before contact, then deliver the barrel on the pitch's plane.

---

Q191. What does 'let the ball travel' really mean?

A. It doesn't mean waiting until the ball is almost past you. The Contact Point note says so directly.

Letting a 95-98 mph outside fastball get too deep makes you late. You get swings and misses, fouls straight back, weak opposite-field flies, or contact near the handle.

What it should mean: don't reach out front. Meet the ball in a consistent window near your front foot, and let pitches come into that window.

The off-speed note uses it the same way. Let the inside fastball travel into the hitting zone and drive it the other way instead of cheating out front.

Q192. Why do I pop up high fastballs?

A. Usually your swing is too steep for the pitch's height.

Common high-pitch mistakes:
- A steep uppercut against a high pitch.
- Dropping the back shoulder too much.
- Standing up to reach the ball.
- Lifting the hands on their own.
- Chasing fastballs above the effective zone.

High fastballs also have backspin that makes them drop less than you expect. If your barrel is under it, you clip the bottom of the ball and pop it up.

Fix: use less shoulder tilt and a flatter barrel path on high pitches, and lay off pitches above about belly-button height.

Q193. Pitchers keep throwing me breaking balls. What should I do?

A. That's a sign they've found a weakness. In the second half of 2025, pitchers attacked Pete Crow-Armstrong's free swinging with nothing but spin, and his average fell to the .190s.

What helps:
- Recognition: watch breakers, call them early, and practice taking ones that finish outside the zone.
- Patience: swing less and hunt one pitch.
- Mechanics: land with your hands back, get on plane early, don't pull your front shoulder off, and don't chop.
- Separation, so you still have time to adjust.

Gunnar Henderson widened his stride by about one foot width and became much better on breaking balls. But he got slightly late on fastballs, so watch for that tradeoff.

Q194. What does the bottom hand do in the swing?

A. The bottom hand, also called the lead hand, is the one closest to the pitcher: the left hand for a right-handed hitter.

It's a guide, like a steering wheel. It:
- Keeps the hands connected to the torso.
- Guides the barrel into the pitch's plane.
- Controls the swing's direction.
- Keeps the hands from leaving the body early.

At contact it should be palm down while the top hand is palm up.

It shouldn't push the bat at the ball on its own. The Bottom-Hand drill trains this: hold a ball in the bottom hand, rotate with your legs and torso, and release in the direction your body turns without reaching with the arm.

Q195. Why is my swing so long?

A. A long swing usually means the barrel gets too far from your body or travels too far behind you before it turns.

Common causes:
- Hands disconnecting from the rear shoulder.
- The barrel drifting behind you early.
- Pushing the hands forward, then swinging the barrel around to catch up.

The Closed-Net drill gives instant feedback. Put a net about three feet behind you. If the bat hits it, your barrel got long.

Don't overcorrect by taking your hands straight at the ball. The goal is short into the zone and long through it: a compact move into the pitch's plane, then a long path through contact.

---

Q196. Why do I hit weak fly balls to the opposite field?

A. The Contact Point note lists weak opposite-field fly balls as one result of letting an outside fastball travel too deep. You end up late and push the ball instead of driving it.

Fix: meet outside pitches near your front-foot plane, not behind it, and let the barrel point toward the opposite field.

Use your legs and back shoulder to reach the outer pitch, not just your arms. Don't pull your front shoulder away.

The BOSU drill and the front-toss progression train this.

Q197. How do pitchers figure out a hitter's weakness?

A. They study location charts and zone results. The battery finds each hitter's weakness and picks a plan for the day.

In the Batter's Mental Game note, charts showed pitchers working Shohei Ohtani low and away, his one cold zone by wOBA (a weighted on-base average). Christian Walker's chart showed him covering the strike zone but weak below it.

Hitters show it in games too. Aaron Judge saw a steady diet of pitches away and sweepers. Pitchers attacked Pete Crow-Armstrong's free swinging with nothing but spin.

Do the same in reverse: know what the pitcher throws, what he finishes with, and how you'll cover your weakness.

---

Q198. What does it take to come through in clutch at-bats?

A. In clutch spots, no pitcher gives you anything easy. The Batter's Mental Game note says better hitters can attack their own weak zone on purpose, either by drawing the pitch there or by predicting it when they know it's coming. It's hard, and big moments demand it anyway.

You also need a plan. Know what the pitcher finishes with and what you're hunting.

Mindset matters too. David Ortiz says the "15%" (the drive to handle adversity) is what actually drives success.

The 2023 World Series example shows the risk: Christian Walker got the 2-0 fastball he expected with two runners in scoring position and did nothing with it.

---

Q199. How much should my upper body coil in my load?

A. There's a wide range, and more isn't always better.

- Large: Juan Soto keeps a big upper-body twist while holding power on his back leg. Separation creates torque.
- Small: Giancarlo Stanton's coil is noticeably smaller than other home run hitters'. His long stride makes up for it.
- None: Luis Arraez builds no upper-body coil and uses weight shift instead.
- Too much: Jung-Hoo Lee's old setup coiled so far that gathering force took too long.

The Roll drill note warns that stretching your hands too far back creates tension and disconnects your arms. Coil only as far as you can stay connected and on time.

---

Q200. What does it mean to 'stay connected' in the swing?

A. It means your hands, arms, rear shoulder, and torso move together instead of separately. It doesn't mean pressing your hands rigidly against your body.

What it looks like:
- In the load and stride, your top hand stays near the rear shoulder.
- At landing, your hands are still back and your rear elbow is organized.
- During the turn, your lead arm moves with your body's rotation instead of racing ahead.

Why it matters: when your hands disconnect, the barrel leaves its track. You get pop-ups, fouls, whiffs, weak contact, and trouble adjusting. Connected hands let your body's rotation carry the barrel into the slot and through the ball.

The Roll drill with a basketball trains this feeling.
